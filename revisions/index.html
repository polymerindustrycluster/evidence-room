What happened after publication

Almost every month of these three price series moved after publication, by almost nothing.

Every chart on this site shows each month at the value it carries today, which hides the Bureau of Labor Statistics revising that figure for months afterwards. This page measures three producer-price series and no others, the prices plants charge for chemicals and for the plastic and rubber goods made from them: 259 of their 273 monthly figures were later changed, by a typical 0.15 percent of the figure first reported, taking the three together.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data ALFRED, the federal archive of every version ever published, 2019–2026 · August 2026

The revisions

Almost every month moved, and almost none moved far

The three series sit on one supply chain. Industrial chemicals is the raw input, the series a buyer quotes when negotiating a resin contract. The other two price the finished goods made from it, and their names differ by one word: rubber and plastic products prices them product by product, plastics and rubber plants prices them plant by plant. All three panels share one scale.

Chemicals and allied products typically moves 0.35% after publication, more than twice as far as either series downstream of it

Each bar is one month. Above the line that month ended higher than the figure first reported, below it lower, and the taller the bar the further it moved. The series that revises hardest is drawn first.

The distribution

How far a number typically travels

If the revisions sat mostly on one side, first estimates would be running consistently high or consistently low, and every trend drawn from fresh data would need a warning. They fall close to evenly on both sides, which is the reassuring half of this page.

Half of all revised months land inside 0.15% of the figure first published, and the largest total revision is 1.41%

259 revised months: 137 ended higher than first reported, 121 ended lower, and one came back to within a thousandth of a point of where it started, which rounds to no change at all.

One month, up close

The month that changed direction

On 14 October 2021 the Bureau of Labor Statistics published its first estimate of September prices for chemicals and allied products: an index reading of 348.8, a level that means nothing on its own. The Bureau republished that month three more times, and it now reads 343.866, which is 1.41 percent lower and the largest total revision on this page. It did not arrive at once: the first of the three corrections did almost all of it, taking 348.8 to 343.934 on 14 December 2021, a single step of 1.40 percent, and the two that followed moved the figure 0.04 percent down and 0.02 percent back up. That it moved in more than one step is a change of Bureau policy rather than anything about this month: effective with that same 14 December 2021 release, and starting with the reference months August to October 2021, the Bureau began republishing interim values every month across the three months between a first print and the recalculation four months later, where before it published that recalculation alone. September 2021 sits at the join, its first print before the change and its last correction after, so how many times a month on this page was republished is not comparable across the window. The year-on-year comparison came through it. In the figures a reader had that October, September stood 24.8 percent above September 2020, and today the same comparison reads 23.1 percent. The month-on-month comparison did not come through it. September read 1.0 percent above August in the October figures, and today it reads 0.3 percent below August. A board deck built that month would have carried the annual figure about right and the monthly one pointing the wrong way.

A fresh figure from these three series is worth quoting for how high it reads, and worth checking against a later version before you quote which way it moved. Nothing in this archive changed more than six months after it was first published, so compare against the version that comes out six months later.
