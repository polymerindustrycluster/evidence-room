Akron and Case Western, 2012–2025

Akron and Case Western have written 208 papers together since 2012. Eight were about polymers.

The University of Akron and Case Western Reserve University are counted here two independent ways, by the papers they publish together and by the federal grants they hold together, across the fourteen years from 2012 to 2025. In those years they coauthored 208 papers, of which eight are classified in polymers, and they hold four joint federal awards worth $3.7 million, the newest of the awards starting in 2017. Joint output peaked at 29 papers in 2018, dropped to 11, and came back to 22 in 2020. It then fell in each of the four years to 2024, down to two papers, and rose to six in 2025.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data OpenAlex works and institutions, NSF Awards API · August 2026

The record

Two hundred and eight papers, and a fall that bottoms out in 2024

Every work in a public index that lists both universities among its author affiliations. Each small mark at the foot of a column is one paper. The lower marks are papers classified under polymers and plastics; the marks above them are papers classified under biomaterials, a separate subject that is counted alongside it and never merged into it. Both are subject classifications the index assigns to a paper, not a match on the word polymer in its text.

Why this page exists

This page replaces an assertion that was never checked: that nothing in the data suggested the polymer chairs at Akron and Case Western, the professors who head each university’s polymer program, had ever been in the same room. That was an unbounded negative, a claim that something has never happened, resting on data that was never about collaboration, and it has been deleted. What the Polymer Industry Cluster (PIC), an initiative of the Greater Akron Chamber, actually has to work with is measured below.

The years counted here stop at 2025, and a large joint award started after they do. Case Western leads NEO-SMART, a regional innovation engine funded by the National Science Foundation, which awarded it on 14 July 2026. The University of Akron is among its core partners, and one of its three research clusters is polymers. That award falls outside this series and is not evidence against it. A reader who already knows about it should not have to wonder whether this page knows about it too.

What this cannot show is an absence. Publication databases and grant records do not see industry contracts, consortium membership, joint teaching or a conversation, so a low count here means only that the work is not visible at this resolution, not that it did not happen.

The control

It is not indexing lag, and it is not only that Akron shrank

A falling count at the end of a series has two innocent explanations before it has an interesting one: the database has not finished indexing recent years, or one partner is simply publishing less of everything. Both are checkable, and both are drawn here rather than argued in a footnote. The lower panel answers the second one by dividing the joint count by Akron’s own output for that year. Read the result as how often the two universities write together for every thousand papers Akron publishes on its own: a higher number means more joint work, a falling number means less.

The money

Four joint federal awards, none starting after 2017 in this window

Federal awards are the second, independent instrument. They are counted two ways, because the National Science Foundation encodes a partnership two ways: as one award whose principal investigator (PI) sits at one university and whose co-principal investigator (co-PI) sits at the other, and as a “Collaborative Research” project split into a separate award for each. Missing the second method undercounts badly. One of the four awards it finds is Northern Ohio AGEP-T, an alliance for graduate education in biology, chemistry and engineering. The series ends in 2025. The NEO-SMART award described above was made in 2026, later than anything this chart can see, and its absence here says nothing about it.

The question was never whether they have met. It is whether they still do.
