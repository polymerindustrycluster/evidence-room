What this is

A cluster checking its own claims in public

The Polymer Industry Cluster, PIC for short, publishes numbers about the plastics, rubber and coatings economy of Northeast Ohio: how many jobs it holds, what they pay, who is available to fill them. A cluster is what its name says, the firms, suppliers and labs of one industry packed into one place. Those numbers used to arrive as bare claims. Here they arrive with the working shown.

Every page is built from federal data that costs nothing to obtain, listed in the source registry below: the payroll census of covered jobs, the file recording where workers live, degree completions, federal contract spending, energy and producer prices. The analysis is scripted rather than hand-assembled, and the analysis and graphics are by Claude (Anthropic), and John Swanson is responsible for what it says. Each reading of what the numbers mean was sent to competing AI systems to be argued down before it was published. Every sentence carrying a number is backed by a check that re-runs against the source data on every build, and each one states the condition that would prove it wrong.

This is working research by PIC staff. It is not official statistics, and it is not a news operation. Where the federal record contradicts something PIC has said, the page says so and the correction is dated in a log that is only ever appended to. Everything here is free to reuse under CC BY 4.0, including the derived data and the scripts, because a figure anyone can re-derive is the only kind worth publishing.

Where does Ohio rank among states, and Akron among metros?

First in the country

Ohio holds 54,846 plastics and rubber jobs, 10,248 more than second-place Texas, with all 51 state geographies disclosed. Akron is 6th of the 155 metros that disclose, where 1st is the largest; 227 metros are withheld and are simply unknown.

Where do the people who work in a county live?

Summit County’s own residents hold 48.4 percent of its 265,460 jobs. Ashtabula is the most self-contained of the twelve and still reaches only 68 percent, so workforce policy set county by county is aimed at people who mostly live somewhere else.

How big is the Tech Hub award next to the federal money that arrives every year?

Ordinary federal contracting in chemicals and plastics across the twelve counties has averaged $36.6 million a year across the seven finished fiscal years, 2019 to 2025, ranging from $17.0 million to $51.6 million. Fiscal 2026 is still running and is not in that average. The $51.0 million Tech Hub award, the federal designation Akron won in October 2023, is about 1.4 years of that average, and fiscal 2019 alone cleared the award line.

24,030 jobs in 2025, a floor rather than a total because the bureau withheld 12 of the 36 county-industry figures. Which polymer industry is this region most concentrated in?

Paint and coatings fill 5.96 times as large a share of local jobs as they do of the country’s, and have led all six polymer industries every year since 2015. Rubber products alone, NAICS 3262, the narrow industry Akron is named for, run 2.44 times; the wider plastics and rubber family that contains it, NAICS 326, runs 2.32 times. Concentration is what makes a region distinctive and what leaves it exposed.

Workforce

How much hiring and separating sits behind a flat job count?

111,529 hires and 111,361 separations across 55 quarters produced 168 net jobs. In the average quarter hires run 10.8 percent of the workforce and separations 10.7 percent, which is how a churn engine that size nets out to nothing.

Can a region rebuild a technician pipeline nobody has sustained?

In 33 years of the federal record, polymer technician programs survive at 27 percent; polymer degree programs and the adjacent trades both hold 48. The layer Northeast Ohio wants to rebuild is the one the record says has never held anywhere.

Before anything leaves the building

Every sentence here is a test that can fail

The fourteen pages here carry 401 checked claims, and 15 of them cannot be machine-checked. Every published sentence is stored as a check that re-runs against the data that produced it, next to the condition that would prove it wrong. The exceptions are quoted from project narratives, press releases and internal registers rather than from datasets, so they name a source and what would prove them wrong but cannot be re-run. Attack those first. More checks is not the same as more truth; it is more places this site can be caught being wrong, which is the only sense in which the number is good news.

Two further pages are built and gated in this repository and are deliberately not linked from here. One is an internal board view of PIC’s own performance: a populated copy would carry member records a public repository with permanent history cannot hold. The other is an unreviewed draft of the public scorecard, held back until communications review answers the question its opening figure turns on, and it says on its own face that it is a draft. Each states its reason in a file at its directory root, neither is counted in the totals above, and putting either in the gallery above is a separate decision that has not been taken.

The commands, for anyone who wants to run them

Re-run every claim, or read what would break it

cd _data/build
python verify_claims.py
python verify_claims.py --list

A failure means a published sentence no longer matches its source. Fix the sentence or the computation, never the check.

Rebuild the data from origin

python fetch_*.py && python derive_*.py

Every dataset is reproducible from _data/build/ with free public APIs. Nothing reads a private table, and nothing writes to production.

Re-running all of them is one command in the repository, python _data/build/verify_claims.py, and the listing doubles as the brief for a hostile reviewer.

The federal sources behind these pages, named once so a reader can find them. QCEW is the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, the payroll count behind every jobs and wage figure here.
