How much of the fall in jobs is the tide. The national industry is falling too, counted on the same three federal industry codes this page counts on: plastics and rubber, resin, and paint and coatings. Private employment in those three across the United States went from 895,431 in 2023 to 857,188 in 2025, down 4.3 percent. The region’s own count of the same three fell 7.2 percent across the same two years, 2023 to 2025, measured on the counties that publish a figure in every year. Those two years are the last two of the run in the headline, which is three falls in a row from 2022 to 2025. Read together: about three-fifths of the region’s pace is the industry’s own decline, because 4.3 divided by 7.2 is 0.6. The region is still falling faster than its industry. Neither half of that reading survives without the other.

How unusual the year was

How far each measure moved, which is a different question from how it is doing

This chart grades nothing. A bar is the SIZE of one step, the move from the year before to the newest year that measure publishes, set against how far that measure usually steps. The colour says whether the measure rose or fell, never whether rising was good. The longest bar belongs to paint concentration, which is how much more paint and coatings work these counties do, per job, than the country does. It is the one measure here where neither direction counts as good news.

Each bar is that measure’s latest change divided by how far it usually moves between years. Dividing lets moves in different units share one axis. In the latest year pay against the national rate moved 0.034, from 0.861 to 0.895 times that rate. Federal contracting moved $16.44 million, from $22.54 million to $38.98 million a year. Both are drawn to the same scale. Past 1.0, the move was bigger than the middle one of that measure’s own earlier moves, which is all “bigger than usual” means here. About half of years clear it. The upright mark on each row is the furthest that measure has ever moved in a single year.

How to read a move

How far these numbers normally move, and what nobody has measured

The yardstick each bar is measured against is built one way for all five measures: jobs, paint concentration, pay against the national rate, polymer degrees and federal contracting. Take the measure’s own published history, work out the change between every consecutive pair of years, hold out the newest one, and compare it against the middle and the largest of the rest. Half a measure’s years land under its own middle by construction, which is why a tile reading “bigger than usual” is a prompt to look rather than a verdict.

That yardstick describes how far a series travels between published years. It says nothing about revision, which is what happens when an agency restates a figure it has already published. The two are different quantities, and it is revision that decides how much weight a brand-new figure can carry. The Evidence Room has measured revision for exactly three producer-price indexes, which track the prices factories charge for what they make. A reference month is one month of one of those indexes, paired with what the index first published for that month. The archived releases cover 259 such months. Across them the middle revision moved the published level by 0.15 percent and the largest moved it by 1.42 percent. For the employment, degree and contracting series on this page, nobody has measured revision at all. The revisions page says so itself and names archived employment releases as the file it needs.

Withheld is not zero. Three of the five measures are built from one employment figure per county per industry, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics withholds a figure when publishing it would identify an employer. The set it withholds changes from year to year, so a raw comparison of two annual totals mixes real change with the bureau’s publication decisions. Where that applies, the direction on this page is read from the figures present in every year.

What would change these readings

For four of the five, the next thing to move them is a data release

A dashboard that never says what would change its own numbers invites the reader to treat them as settled. Each of these is a live federal series with a known refresh. The polymer degree count is the slow one: the newest year it publishes is older than the newest year of every other measure here.
