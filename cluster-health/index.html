How unusual the year was

How far each measure moved, which is a different question from how it is doing

This chart grades nothing. A bar is the SIZE of this year’s step against how far that measure usually steps, and the colour says whether the measure rose or fell, not whether rising was good. The longest bar belongs to concentration, the one measure on this page with no better end at all.

Each bar is this year’s change divided by how far that measure usually moves between years, so a three-hundredths move in a wage ratio and a $16 million move in contracting land on one axis. Past 1.0 the move beat the middle of that measure’s own earlier moves, which is what “bigger than usual” means here and which about half of years do. The upright mark on each row is the furthest that measure has ever moved in a single year.

The five measures

Each one, the baseline it is read against, and what it cannot see

We have set no target for any of these five, so no tile reports distance from one. Each reports a level, where that level sits in the measure’s own published range, which end of that range is the better one for the region where there is one, a direction against a stated baseline, and the page it came from, where the working is shown in full.

How to read a move

How far these numbers normally move, and what nobody has measured

The yardstick each bar is measured against is built one way for all five measures. Take the measure’s own published history, work out the change between every consecutive pair of years, hold out the newest one, and compare it against the middle and the largest of the rest. Half a measure’s years land under its own middle by construction, which is why a badge saying a move beat it is a prompt to look rather than a verdict.

That yardstick describes how far a series travels between published years. It says nothing about revision, and the difference decides how much weight a fresh figure can carry. A revision measure would say how far the year just published will move once the agencies restate it, and this repository has measured that for exactly three producer-price indexes, the prices factories charge for what they make: 259 reference months of archived earlier releases, a middle revision of 0.15 percent of the level and a largest of 1.42 percent. For the employment, degree and spending series on this page, nobody has measured it. The revisions page says so itself and names archived employment releases as the file it needs.

Withheld is not zero. Three of the five measures are built from one employment figure per county per industry, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics withholds a figure when publishing it would identify an employer. The set it withholds changes from year to year, so a raw comparison of two annual totals mixes real change with the bureau’s publication decisions. Where that applies, the direction on this page is read from the figures present in every year.
