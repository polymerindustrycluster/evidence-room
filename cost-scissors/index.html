Polymer Industry Cluster Operating conditions · the price chain

Feedstock, resin and product prices, indexed to January 2019

Natural gas round-tripped completely. Finished polymer products are at an all-time high this month.

The 2022 price spike is over at the top of the chain and has barely moved at the bottom of it, and the pattern in between is a clean ladder. Measure how much of each price's rise has since come back and the answer falls monotonically with distance from the wellhead: gas has given back everything, crude about half, resin about a third, and finished polymer products nothing at all. An earlier version of this page showed only the two ends and could describe the gap. Three stages explain where in the chain it stuck.

The finding

How much of each price rise has come back

Each bar starts at the series' own peak since January 2019 and runs back toward its base. A full bar means the price has completely round-tripped; an empty one means it is still sitting at its high. Nothing about this ordering was designed — it is what the four stages do.

The whole chain

Every series on one axis, because every series is rebased

These are dollars per thousand cubic feet, cents per kilowatt-hour and three different index bases — quantities that must never share a value axis. Indexing each to its own January 2019 makes one axis honest, and the axis then reads as percent change rather than as price.

The spread

What a converter buys against what a converter sells

One index subtracted from the other: finished-product prices minus resin prices, both rebased to the same month. Above zero means output prices have risen further than input prices since 2019. This is the single line most relevant to a PIC member, and it is the one most easily misread — the note underneath says why.

Input costs normalized. Output prices did not, and the gap is now the widest it has been.
