Prices along the polymer chain, 2015 to 2026

Natural gas gave the whole spike back. Finished polymer products are at a record high, before inflation.

Gas has given back its whole 2022 spike, crude oil about half, resin about a third, and finished polymer products none of it. Those are cash prices; after inflation the chain still runs feedstock, then resin, then product, though crude and resin change places inside it. Either way the 2022 spike has not unwound evenly, and the prices nearest the customer have held. The cluster has members on every link.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data from the federal energy and producer-price series, January 2015 to July 2026 · August 2026

The finding

In July 2026 each step away from the wellhead had kept more of the 2022 rise, except the power bill

In posted prices, gas gave back all of its run-up and by July cost less than before the spike; crude oil gave back about half, resin about a third, and finished polymer products none at all. Deflate the same prices by the general price level and the middle of that order changes hands: resin has given back its whole real rise, about 101 percent of it, crude about three-quarters, and finished products about a quarter rather than none. The chain still runs feedstock, then resin, then product either way, which is the ordering this page rests on. It is the latest month’s order, not a fixed one: it held in 29 of the 43 months from January 2023 and broke in four of the last eight, most recently in April. One feedstock breaks the pattern: Ohio industrial electricity, the one series whose record stops in May 2026 rather than July, peaked in January 2026, not in 2022, and by May had given back only about a quarter, which is why it sits out of order in the chart below, its May reading beside the others’ July. Feedstock here means whatever the plants buy at the front of the chain, so the power bill sits beside the gas and the crude even though resin is not made from it. And four of the seven prices come in pairs: the federal statistics measure resin twice and finished products twice, once from the industries that make them and once as goods traded on the open market. Every number this page later subtracts uses the makers’ pair.

Where do you sit?

Gas gave back its whole rise and then some; finished products gave back none

Each bar is one price. The longer the bar, the more of its run-up has come back off. A full bar means the price is back to what it cost in January 2019; past the line, its latest price is below where the run-up started. Gas is the worked example: on the scale in the next chart it peaked at 283 and read 93 in July 2026, a fall of 190 against the 183 points it had gained above 100: 104% given back, which is why its bar runs past the line.

The whole chain

The spike moved down the chain and stopped at the finished product

Henry Hub natural gas, the Louisiana pipeline junction whose price is the national benchmark, rose to nearly three times its January 2019 price by August 2022, an index of 283, and fell all the way back. West Texas Intermediate crude, the benchmark American oil, crested that June and kept about half of its rise. Resin crested across 2021 and 2022 and in July 2026 still held about two thirds of its rise. The two finished-product lines climbed to about 40% above their January 2019 price and had not come down by then: the wave weakened at every link until the last one, where it set.

Feedstock spiked and fell; product prices climbed and stayed

Each line is one price, with January 2019 set to 100: a line at 140 costs 40% more than it did then, and a line under 100 costs less. Color: one series per chain link. Gray: related series, for context (each one named, with the value it ends at, in the key below the chart).

The gap

From 22.5 points under resin to 14.3 over

The line below tracks how far the price a parts maker charges (a converter, in the trade: the plant that buys resin and moulds or extrudes it into finished parts) has run ahead of the price it pays for resin. Both are counted as growth since January 2019, so +14.3 means product prices have risen 14.3 percentage points more than resin prices, and below zero means resin rose more. That was the converter’s world through the shortage, bottoming 22.5 points under in August 2021. The line crossed zero in August 2022 by eight hundredths of a point, stayed above it, and in January 2026 reached +22.5, as far above zero as the 2021 trough sat below it. It has not held there. Resin rose 23.2 points between March and May 2026, nearly a quarter of its 2019 base, the gap closed to +0.7, its thinnest month since September 2022, and it reopened to +14.3 in July. Three of the forty-eight months since the crossing have come in under a single point: the crossing itself at +0.08 in August 2022, +0.58 the month after it, and +0.71 in May 2026. All three sit inside the revision band this site has measured for these indexes: on the archived-vintage record behind the revisions page, a published month of the product series moves by 0.10 percent of its level at the median, which is 0.14 of a point on this scale, so the crossing is smaller than a routine revision to one of the two legs it subtracts. The gray line asks the same question one link up, resin against chemicals and allied products, the broad index resin sits inside: a +19.2-point gap in September 2021 that has since unwound to just below zero.

Below zero through the shortage, above it since 2022, and three times by less than a point

Above the zero line, product prices have grown more since 2019 than resin prices; below it, resin has grown more. One point is one percentage point of growth: an index up 30% against one up 20% is a gap of ten points. Gray: the same question one link up, resin against chemicals and allied products.

This is not a margin. These selling-price indexes do not separately measure labor, freight, energy, tooling, packaging or a producer’s full costs. A widening gap is consistent with flat or falling profitability. Two readings fit and the data cannot separate them: the industry held price after input costs fell, or its other costs rose enough to require it. Anyone quoting this line owes the reader both.

One part, priced through it

What the scissors did to a dollar part

The scissors is the page’s namesake: two blades moving apart, the cost of inputs rising as one and the price of the finished part as the other. The indexes measure the blades, not the margin between them. Take a part that left an Ohio molding line at $1.00 in January 2019 and price it at the industry average ever since. In August 2021 it billed about $1.14 while the resin going into it cost 36% more than in 2019: the part up 14, resin up 36, which is the 22.5-point trough on the chart above. In July 2026 the same part billed about $1.40, its resin was up 25%, and the gas behind that resin cost 7% less than it did in 2019. The dollars here are rounded to the cent and the percentages to the point, so they subtract to about the gap, never exactly to it. The part is illustrative, priced by the industry index rather than any member’s quote, and its arc is the page in miniature: its price trailed resin on the way up and held after resin fell back.

What would sharpen this band: one member’s own quoted-price history for a single part, January 2019 to now. The desk does not have one, so nothing on this page is a member’s number, and the dollar figures below are the industry average translated into dollars.
