Prices along the polymer chain, 2015 to 2026

Natural gas round-tripped. Finished polymer products are at an all-time high this month.

Gas has given back its whole 2022 spike, crude oil about half, resin about a third, and finished polymer products none of it. That ordering means somebody in this chain ate the spike and somebody banked it, and the cluster has members on every link.

By the Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics J. Swanson · Data from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) and the producer price indexes the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) publishes through the Federal Reserve’s FRED service, January 2015 to July 2026 · August 2026

The finding

Each step away from the wellhead kept more of the 2022 rise — except the power bill

Gas gave back all of its run-up and now costs less than before the spike; crude oil gave back about half, resin about a third, and finished polymer products none at all. One feedstock breaks the pattern: Ohio industrial electricity peaked this January, not in 2022, and has given back only about a quarter, which is why it sits out of order in the chart below. Feedstock here means whatever the plants buy at the front of the chain, so the power bill sits beside the gas and the crude even though resin is not made from it. And four of the seven prices come in pairs: the federal statistics measure resin twice and finished products twice, once from the industries that make them and once as goods traded on the open market. Every number this page later subtracts uses the makers’ pair.

Where do you sit?

Gas gave back its whole rise and then some; finished products gave back none

Each bar is one price. The longer the bar, the more of its run-up has come back off. A full bar means the price is back to what it cost in January 2019; past the line, it costs less now than before the run-up started. Gas is the worked example: on the scale in the next chart it peaked at 283 and now reads 93, a fall of 190 against the 183 points it had gained above 100 — 104% given back, which is why its bar runs past the line.

The whole chain

The spike moved down the chain and stopped at the finished product

Henry Hub natural gas — the Louisiana pipeline junction whose price is the national benchmark — rose to nearly three times its January 2019 price by August 2022, an index of 283, and fell all the way back. West Texas Intermediate crude, the benchmark American oil, crested that June and kept about half of its rise. Resin crested across 2021 and 2022 and still holds about two thirds of its rise. The two finished-product lines climbed to about 40% above their January 2019 price and have not come down: the wave weakened at every link until the last one, where it set.

Feedstock spiked and fell; product prices climbed and stayed

Each line is one price, with January 2019 set to 100: a line at 140 costs 40% more than it did then, and a line under 100 costs less. Color: one series per chain link. Gray: related series, for context — each one named, with the value it ends at, in the key below the chart.

The gap

The squeeze became a cushion: 22.5 points under resin, now 14.3 over

The line below tracks how far the price a parts maker charges — a converter, in the trade: the plant that buys resin and moulds or extrudes it into finished parts — has run ahead of the price it pays for resin. Both are counted as growth since January 2019, so +14.3 means product prices have risen 14.3 percentage points more than resin prices, and below zero means resin rose more. That was the converter’s world through the shortage, bottoming 22.5 points under in August 2021. The line crossed zero in August 2022 by eight hundredths of a point, stayed above it, and in January 2026 reached +22.5 — as far above zero as the 2021 trough sat below it. It has not held there. Resin rose 23.2 points between March and May 2026, nearly a quarter of its 2019 base, the cushion closed to +0.7, its thinnest month since September 2022, and it reopened to +14.3 in July. Three of the forty-eight months since the crossing have come in under a single point: the crossing itself at +0.08 in August 2022, +0.58 the month after it, and +0.71 in May 2026. The gray line asks the same question one link up, resin against the industrial chemicals that feed it: a +19.2 windfall in September 2021 that has since unwound to just below zero.

Below zero through the squeeze, above it since 2022, and three times by less than a point

Above the zero line, product prices have grown more since 2019 than resin prices; below it, resin has grown more. One point is one percentage point of growth: an index up 30% against one up 20% is a gap of ten points. Gray: the same question one link up, resin against industrial chemicals.

This is not a margin. Labor, freight, energy, tooling and packaging are in neither index, and several of those rose sharply over exactly this period; a widening gap is entirely consistent with flat or falling profitability. Two readings fit and the data cannot separate them: the industry held price after input costs fell, or its other costs rose enough to require it. Anyone quoting this line owes the reader both.

One part, priced through it

What the scissors did to a dollar part

Take a part that left an Ohio molding line at $1.00 in January 2019 and price it at the industry average ever since. In August 2021 it billed about $1.14 while the resin going into it cost 36% more than in 2019: the part up 14, resin up 36, which is the squeeze — the 22.5-point trough on the chart above, absorbed at the machine. Today the same part bills about $1.40, its resin is up 25%, and the gas behind that resin costs 7% less than it did in 2019. The dollars here are rounded to the cent and the percentages to the point, so they subtract to about the gap, never exactly to it. The part is illustrative, priced by the industry index rather than any member’s quote, and its arc is the page in miniature: it paid for the spike on the way up and kept the price after the spike left.

What would sharpen this band: one member’s own quoted-price history for a single part, January 2019 to now. The desk does not have one, so nothing on this page is a member’s number, and the dollar figures below are the industry average translated into dollars.
