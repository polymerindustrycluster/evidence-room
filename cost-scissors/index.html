Polymer Industry Cluster Operating conditions · the price chain

Feedstock, resin and product prices, indexed to January 2019

Natural gas round-tripped completely. Finished polymer products are at an all-time high this month.

Gas has given back its whole 2022 spike, crude oil about half, resin about a third, and finished polymer products none of it. That ordering, drawn from eleven years of federal price series, means somebody in this chain ate the spike and somebody banked it. Which one you were depends on the link you sell from, and the cluster has members on every link.

The finding

Each step away from the wellhead kept more of the 2022 rise

Each bar measures how much of a series’ rise above January 2019 has since come back. Gas gave back all of it and now costs less than before the spike; crude oil gave back about half, resin about a third, and finished polymer products none at all. One feedstock breaks the pattern: Ohio industrial electricity peaked this January, not in 2022, and has given back only about a quarter. The series are public EIA and BLS data, the analysis is scripted from them, and every published number on this page is re-checked against the data on each build.

The whole chain

The spike moved down the chain and stopped at the finished product

Gas tripled by August 2022 and fell all the way back. Crude crested that June and kept about half of its rise. Resin crested across 2021 and 2022 and still holds about two thirds of its rise. The two finished-product lines climbed to about 40% above their base and have not come down: the wave weakened at every link until the last one, where it set.

The spread

A 23-point squeeze has become a 14-point cushion

One index minus the other: finished-product prices minus resin prices, both rebased to the same month. Below zero, output prices had risen less since 2019 than input prices, and that was the converter’s world through the shortage, bottoming 23 points under water in August 2021. The line crossed zero in August 2022, has stayed above it every month since, and peaked at +23 this January before easing to +14. The gray line asks the same question one link up, resin against the industrial chemicals that feed it: a +19 windfall in September 2021 that has since unwound to just below zero.

Below zero through the squeeze, above zero every month since August 2022

Finished-product index minus resin index, in points; both series set to 100 at January 2019. Gray: the same computation one link up (resin minus industrial chemicals).

This is not a margin. Labor, freight, energy, tooling and packaging are in neither index, and several of those rose sharply over exactly this period; a widening spread is entirely consistent with flat or falling profitability. Two readings fit and the data cannot separate them: the industry held price after input costs fell, or its other costs rose enough to require it. Anyone quoting this line owes the reader both.

One part, priced through it

What the scissors did to a dollar part

Take a part that left an Ohio molding line at $1.00 in January 2019 and price it at the industry average ever since. In August 2021 it billed about $1.14 while the resin going into it cost 36% more than in 2019: that is the squeeze, absorbed at the machine. Today the same part bills about $1.40, its resin is up 25%, and the gas behind that resin costs 7% less than it did in 2019. The part is illustrative, priced by the industry index rather than any member’s quote, and its arc is the page in miniature: it paid for the spike on the way up and kept the price after the spike left.
