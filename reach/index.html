Polymer research from Akron and Case Western, 2015–2024

The region’s polymer research reaches more than 600 institutions in 50 countries, and four papers in five are led from here.

Across ten years these papers carry co-authors at national laboratories, at a polymer company in Akron, and at universities on five continents. A single collaboration count cannot separate two questions: who does the region bring in, and who brings the region in?

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data OpenAlex works and institutions · August 2026

The network

Every institution on a polymer paper with Akron or Case Western

Two rules decide what is counted. The split is by corresponding author, not first author, because in this field the first author is normally the graduate student who ran the experiment while the corresponding author is the investigator whose lab it is. And the bound is a classification, not a keyword: an earlier version of this page matched the word “polymer” in the text and returned 4,037 papers, four fifths of whose largest partners were hospitals doing biomedical polymer research, a different field and a different cluster. The subject classification for polymers and plastics leaves 1,448. One mark per institution, placed where it is, sized by how many papers it shares with the region. Color says which way the work flowed: teal where a corresponding author sat here, orange where the region joined a project someone else was leading. Institutions appearing on a single paper are left off the map and kept in the table, because at this scale one shared paper is a dot the eye cannot price.

Reach and standing are different things. A region that always joins other people’s projects has collaborators but not gravity; one that always leads has gravity but may not travel. Both counts are shown for every major partner, and neither is subtracted from the other.

Whether it lands

A quarter of these papers sit in the top tenth of their field

Collaboration is not impact, and this section answers its own question rather than being laid beside the last one. Raw citation counts are not used: a 2015 paper has had a decade to accumulate them and a 2024 paper has had months, so a raw trend would draw the calendar. This is field-weighted citation impact, which normalises for both field and age: 1.0 is the world average for a paper of that kind and vintage.

A cluster that publishes with fifty countries is not a regional asset that happens to be here.
