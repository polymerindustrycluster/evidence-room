Is this workforce stable, or only flat? · Census QWI, 2012–2025

The region recorded 111,500 hires to end up with 168 more jobs.

Every employment chart PIC publishes shows a stock: how many jobs exist. Underneath that flat line, plastics and rubber manufacturing in these twelve counties starts and ends about a fifth of itself every year. These are job events, not people: a Summit-to-Stark move counts twice. What follows is how big that movement is, which way it has turned since 2012, and what it looks like on a 150-person floor.

The flow

Hires above the line, separations below, for 55 quarters

Both are flows: jobs starting and ending during the quarter. The line through the middle is the net, which is what a conventional employment chart shows on its own.

The rate

Turnover has risen since 2012 and has not come back down

Churn rate is the average of hires and separations over the jobs that exist: the share of the workforce that moved in or out that quarter. The pandemic spike is visible, and so is the fact that the line settles above where it started.

Where the turnover concentrates

A high rate on a small base is a different problem from a high rate on a large one, so this reads on two axes at once: the rate a county runs, and the number of jobs it runs it on. Twelve counties are twelve readings of the same measure by the same method, and they are the only cross-sectional comparison this pull supports.
