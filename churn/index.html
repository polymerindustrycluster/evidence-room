Job flows against headcount · Census QWI, 2012–2025

A tenth of this workforce turns over every quarter. Almost none of it is growth.

Two Census measures answer two different questions. Are there more jobs? No: the headcount fell by 719, to 17,725. How much movement is under that? One job in ten here starts and one in ten ends every quarter: about 14 in and 14 out at a 150-person plant. The ledger nets them: 111,529 hires, 111,361 separations, +168. They are job events, not people: a Summit-to-Stark move counts twice.

Is a tenth of the jobs, every quarter, a lot? No. Three of the six biggest plastics and rubber states churn faster than this region and three churn slower. What is unusual is which way the movement runs. A payroll churns when people leave for better work, and it churns when a plant cannot keep anyone. The rise since 2012 is almost all on the leaving side: the separation rate, the share of jobs that end in a quarter, rose 1.9 points; the hire rate rose 0.2. Since 2023Q1 more jobs have ended than started every quarter, the second kind of churn, the bad one.

Can a sum of counties be set beside a whole state? This page used to say summing counties inflates its flows by an amount the Census cannot measure. It is measurable. Summing every Ohio county the Census discloses for this industry in 2025Q2 gives a hire rate of 10.4% against the 10.6% Ohio publishes as one unit, and the county sum falls short of the state total rather than exceeding it, by about the employment in the 17 Ohio counties the Census withholds. Summing does not inflate the flows.

The rate

Turnover sits above its 2012 level and has fallen every quarter since its 2022 peak

Churn rate is the share of jobs that start or end in a quarter: at 10%, about one job in ten starts and another one in ten ends. Higher means more movement through the same payroll, not a gain or loss of jobs. Three readings of it run through this page, and they are not in conflict: 10.8% is the average across all 55 published quarters, 8.5% is where 2012 sat, and 9.6% is the last four published quarters. The pandemic spike is visible, and so is the long fall off it: the yearly average has come down in every one of the 13 quarters since it peaked at 16.7% in 2022, and it still ends above where it started.

The yearly average has fallen every quarter since 2022 and still ends 1.1 points above 2012, at 9.6%

Which side moved

The extra turnover is people leaving

The rate above averages the two flows, hires and separations, and only one of them moved. Between 2012 and the last four published quarters the separation rate rose about 1.9 points; the hire rate rose about 0.2. In 2012, hires ran 0.9 points ahead of separations. Averaged over a year, separations have run ahead in every quarter since 2023Q1.

Who is nearest the door

More than a quarter of these jobs are held by someone 55 or older

The same Census series splits the same twelve counties into eight age bands. Two things fall out of it. The workforce is old and getting older: the share of jobs held by someone 55 or older has gone from about 22 in every 100 in 2012 to about 28 today. And hiring collapses with age: from the 35-to-44 band on, more jobs end each quarter than start, so the churn on this page is not one rate spread evenly across a payroll.

Hiring falls nearly tenfold with age, and past 65 jobs end at more than twice the rate they start

One row per age band, 2024Q4 to 2025Q3, the twelve counties summed. The share beside each band is its share of the jobs the Census discloses by age. The 14-to-18 band is not drawn: it is one job in a thousand here, it starts 74.7% of its jobs and ends 53.7% of them, twice the next band and off the right of this scale, and it rests on 11 of its 48 possible county-quarter cells. Its row is in the table. The 65-and-older band rests on 29 of its 48 possible cells, so read it for direction rather than to the tenth; the six between them run 42 to 48 of 48. Withheld cells leave 2.3% of jobs outside the eight bands, which is why the 55-and-older share is given as a range below.

One rate is not everyone’s rate. The 19-to-21 band starts or ends a third of its jobs every quarter, 33.6%. The 55-to-64 band moves about one job in twenty, 4.8%. Both sit inside the region’s 9.6%. Age is the only such split this series carries: the Quarterly Workforce Indicators record no occupation at all, so nothing here says whether a molding-machine operator, the person who runs the machine that shapes the part, turns over faster than a maintenance technician. Read 9.6% as the rate for the payroll as a whole and never as the rate on any one kind of job. That question needs a different instrument.

How much of the movement is age? About 4,000 of these jobs are held by someone between 55 and 64. Spread evenly across those ten years, about 400 reach 65 in any year, which is 2.2% of the region’s 17,943 plastics and rubber jobs. That is an estimate and not a measurement: the Census records separations, not retirements, and a separation at 64 is the same record as one at 34. It is at least the right size. The 65-and-older band recorded 394 separations across these four quarters, which is what a flow of about 400 a year into it would have to support.

On a shop floor

What 9.6% a quarter looks like at a 150-person plant

Over the last four published quarters the cluster moved 9.6% of its jobs in a quarter. At a 150-person plant that is about 14 people starting and 14 leaving every quarter, roughly five of each in a month, and about 58 replacement hires to make in a year. Across all 55 quarters the rate averages 10.8%, which works out to 16 and 16 a quarter and about 65 replacement hires a year. The plant is illustrative: it applies a cluster average to a single site, and no single plant runs at the average.

A plant’s hiring is not the region’s training demand. Those 58 hires are one plant’s burden, and most of them are filled by people already working in plastics and rubber who moved from another employer. What a training pipeline has to supply is a different and much smaller quantity: people leaving the industry for good, plus growth. Age alone accounts for about three of the plant’s 58, and about 400 of the 6,626 hires the whole region makes in a year. Treat that 400 as a floor rather than the answer. People who leave plastics and rubber for a job in another industry also have to be replaced, and this series cannot see them: telling those apart needs the Census’s Job-to-Job Flows, a different product this page does not carry.

Flat is what fast looks like from a distance.

Between 2012Q1 and 2025Q3 the cluster recorded 222,890 hire and separation events and finished 168 hires ahead of separations: about 1,327 events for every one job of that margin. That margin is a ledger of flows, and it is not the answer to whether the industry has more jobs. The headcount is, and the headcount fell by 719, about 4%. Net employment is a legitimate outcome measure, and funders are entitled to score it. The point is that a program changing how people move through this industry can do real work without moving that number, and that since 2023Q1 the movement has run toward the door.
