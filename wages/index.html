Do polymer jobs beat their towns?

The middle polymer pairing pays 1.2 times what the average job in its county pays.

A pairing is one polymer industry in one county: ask what that work pays against the average job in that same county. Polymer wins in 40 of the 51 pairings big enough for the Bureau of Labor Statistics to publish, and all eleven it loses make plastics or rubber. Behind those 51 pairings are 33,528 jobs, and two of the six industries tracked here are groups holding the other four, so counting pairings is not the same as counting jobs.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data: the Bureau of Labor Statistics Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW), annual averages 2015–2025 · August 2026

The finding

Where polymer pay beats the town, and where it doesn’t

Every figure here rests on one ratio: what polymer work pays in a county, against what the average job in that same county pays. At 1.0× the two are equal; at 1.2× polymer pay is a fifth higher; at 0.9× it is a tenth lower. (The arithmetic: the industry’s average weekly wage in a county, divided by that county’s average across every industry.) It is the checkable version of the region’s good-jobs claim. The data are that census, which counts every job covered by unemployment insurance, so it holds almost all payroll work and none of the self-employed, and every published number here is re-checked against it on each build.

What an average weekly wage hides. It tracks hours and the mix of jobs alongside pay rates: a plant on overtime reads as a raise, and a manager and a line operator average into one number. Each number is an average over one published pairing, so one large employer can move a county. It answers “what does this work pay here, relative to everything else here,” not “what would you earn.”

The split

The premium is a chemistry premium

Chemistry-side pairings pay about a third more than their county’s average job, a median 1.35×, and every one of the 23 clears it. Plastics-and-rubber pairings come out level with their towns, a median 1.01×, with 17 of 28 above. One plausible explanation: chemicals, resin and paint are capital-intensive, continuous processes (more output per worker, round-the-clock operator shifts, more chemists per line), while molding and extrusion compete with every other light manufacturer in town for the same labor.

The counterweight

The same jobs trail their own industries nationally

Beating the county average is the local test. The national one is harder: 44 of the 51 pairings pay less than the same industry pays elsewhere in the country, the typical one about 12 percent less (0.88×). Both facts are true at once, and the recruiting pitch that survives scrutiny carries both.

Nothing lands in the top-left corner: no pairing pays under its own town while beating its own industry. Lake County chemicals is the far-right dot, 2.09× its county and 1.04× its industry. The other counterweight

Against the factory next door, polymer pay is level

The 1.21× premium is real, and it is not particular to polymer. Manufacturing as a whole out-pays the all-jobs average in every one of the twelve counties, by a median 1.25×. Measure the same 51 pairings against their county’s manufacturing wage instead of its all-jobs average and the middle pairing lands at 0.99×: dead level with the factory next door, 23 of the 51 above it.

Both comparisons answer a real question, and they answer different ones. Against the town, polymer work pays like factory work, and factory work pays well; that is the sentence a recruiter can use. Against the factories, polymer pay is ordinary. A pitch that rests on polymer paying specially is resting on the first comparison while sounding like the second, and the gap between them is the whole 1.25× that manufacturing at large carries.

Over time

A decade on, the premium has held

Every year since 2015 the middle pairing has paid roughly a tenth to a quarter more than its county’s average job, a median premium between 1.12× and 1.25×, ending at 1.21× against 1.20× at the start. The honest reading is flat: the cluster is not falling toward its surroundings, and it is not pulling away from them either. The year-to-year moves are small next to the spread across pairings.

What it means in a paycheck

The top of the chart is a $129,000 job

A Lake County chemical-manufacturing job averages $2,474 a week, about $129,000 a year, in a county where the average job pays about $62,000. Same county, the other family: rubber-products work averages $1,028 a week, about $53,000, below what the average job there pays. And the lowest pairing on the page, Stark County rubber products at $853 a week, about $44,000, covers 30 jobs, a reminder that the smallest pairings here average very few paychecks. Every number here averages a whole pairing rather than describing any one person’s pay.

The premium is real against these towns, and a discount against these industries.
