Do polymer jobs out-pay the towns they sit in? · BLS QCEW, 2015–2025

The typical polymer job pays 1.2 times what the average job in its county pays.

In 40 of the 51 industry-county pairings the Bureau of Labor Statistics can disclose, polymer pay beats the county's all-industry average — and the eleven that miss all sit on one side of the house. That is the checkable number behind the region's good-jobs pitch: the wage argument members take to commissioners, school boards and recruits.

The finding

Where polymer pay beats the town — and where it doesn't

A pairing is one industry's average weekly wage in one county, divided by that county's average across every industry — above 1.0×, polymer work out-pays its town. The data are the BLS census of covered jobs, the analysis is scripted from the public files, and every published number on this page is re-checked against the data on each build.

What an average weekly wage hides. It moves with hours and the mix of jobs, not only pay rates — a plant on overtime reads as a raise, and a manager and a line operator average into one number. These are means per disclosed pairing, so one large employer can move a county. It answers “what does this work pay here, relative to everything else here” — not “what would you earn.”

The split

The premium is a chemistry premium

Chemistry-side pairings run a median 1.35× their county's average, and every one of the 23 clears it. Plastics-and-rubber pairings run a median 1.01×, with 17 of 28 above. One plausible explanation: chemicals, resin and paint are capital-intensive, continuous processes — more output per worker, round-the-clock operator shifts, more chemists per line — while molding and extrusion compete with every other light manufacturer in town for the same labor.

The counterweight

The same jobs trail their own industries nationally

Beating the county average is the local test. The national one is harder: 44 of the 51 pairings pay below what the same industry averages across the United States — the median sits at 0.88×. Both facts are true at once, and the recruiting pitch that survives scrutiny carries both.

Over time

A decade on, the premium has held

The median premium has stayed between 1.12× and 1.25× since 2015, ending at 1.21× against 1.20× at the start. The honest reading is flat: the cluster is not falling toward its surroundings, and it is not pulling away from them either — a year-to-year move of a point or two is small next to the spread across pairings.

What it means in a paycheck

2.09× is a life, not a ratio

A Lake County chemical-manufacturing job averages $2,474 a week — about $129,000 a year — in a county where the average job pays about $62,000. Same county, other side of the house: rubber-products work averages $1,028 a week, about $53,000, under the county line. And the lowest pairing on the page — Stark County rubber products, $853 a week, about $44,000 — is 30 jobs, a reminder that the small cells here are averages of very few paychecks. These are cell averages, not any one person's pay.

Against its own towns, the wage claim holds. Against its own industries, it is still a discount.
