Wages against local prices, 2023

The same paycheck ranks below average in dollars and above average in what it buys.

Hiring against Chicago money, the offer number is half the argument: the same paycheck stretches about 10 percent further in Akron. Among the 56 metros with a serious polymer workforce, Akron is 33rd on the printed number and 19th on what it buys, a smaller climb than PIC usually pitches.

The flip

Where each polymer metro sits before and after the price adjustment

Every metro with at least 2,000 polymer jobs, best paid at the top: ranked on the left by the wage printed on the paycheck, on the right by what that wage buys. A line sloping down means a metro’s wages buy less than the dollar figure suggests. The steepest falls are the places PIC competes with for people.

Every wage comparison PIC has ever published is nominal. Nominal means the number printed on the paycheck, before any adjustment for what things cost where you live; its opposite here is “real,” what the paycheck buys. The Bureau of Economic Analysis prices the same national basket in every metro and scores each one against the country: 100 is the national average, under 100 means things cost less than they do nationally, over 100 means more. Akron scores 92.9, so prices here run about 7 percent below the US average. Those parities cover metropolitan areas only, so the region appears here as the Akron metro area alone: Cleveland and Canton have no published 2023 polymer wage.

The honest counterweight

Northeast Ohio is not a cheap metro. It is an average one.

The advantage above is real, but it does not come from where PIC usually says it does. Akron’s price level is almost exactly the metro median, and more than a hundred metros are cheaper. What produces the climb is that a large share of the polymer industry sits in genuinely expensive places.

The median is the middle value, with as many above as below, rather than the average, which a few very high earners can pull upward.

The two things a recruiter is actually trading off

The further right a metro sits, the bigger the paycheck. The further up, the more that paycheck has to cover. So the top left is the worst trade a recruiter can make and the bottom right the best. Every metro on one dashed diagonal ends up with the same buying power.

What it means in an offer letter

Against Chicago, the price advantage is worth about $6,600 a year

Akron’s average polymer paycheck is $1,238 a week, about $64,000 a year. To hand a candidate the same buying power, a Chicago employer has to write about $71,000, and the spread widens up the price ladder: matching it in San Francisco takes about $81,000. These are averages of covered paychecks in each metro, not any one person’s offer; the point is the size of the wedge a recruiter can put in a candidate’s spreadsheet.

A Chicago offer has to clear $71,000 to beat Akron’s $64,000.
