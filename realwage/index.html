Wages against local prices, 2023

The same paycheck ranks below the middle metro in dollars and above it in what it buys.

When Akron hires against Chicago money, the offer number is half the argument: the same dollars stretch about 10 percent further in Akron than in Chicago. Among the 56 metros with at least 2,000 polymer jobs, Akron is 33rd on the printed number and 19th on what it buys, a climb of 14 places.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data: the federal census of jobs and pay (BLS QCEW) and metro price levels (BEA Regional Price Parities) · August 2026

The flip

Where each polymer metro sits before and after the price adjustment

Every metro with at least 2,000 polymer jobs, best paid at the top: ranked on the left by the wage printed on the paycheck, on the right by what that wage buys. A line sloping down means a metro’s wages buy less than the dollar figure suggests. The steepest falls are the places PIC competes with for people.

A polymer job here means one in plastics and rubber products manufacturing, industry code NAICS 326. Every wage comparison PIC has ever published is nominal. Nominal means the number printed on the paycheck, before any adjustment for what things cost where you live; its opposite here is “real,” what the paycheck buys. The Bureau of Economic Analysis prices the same shopping list of goods and services in every metro and scores each one against the country: 100 is the national average, under 100 means things cost less than they do nationally, over 100 means more. Akron scores 92.9, so prices here run about 7 percent below the US average. Those price levels are published for metropolitan areas only, so PIC’s twelve-county footprint cannot be scored at all: the region appears here as the Akron metro area alone, and Cleveland and Canton have no published 2023 polymer wage.

The honest counterweight

Northeast Ohio sits in the middle of the metro price range.

PIC’s standard recruiting line has been that Akron is a low-cost place to live and hire. The price data will not carry it: Akron’s price level is almost exactly the metro median, and more than a hundred metros are cheaper. The advantage above is real, and what produces it is that a large share of the polymer industry sits in genuinely expensive places.

The median is the middle value, with as many above as below, rather than the average, which a few very high earners can pull upward.

One tick per metro, cheapest on the left and most expensive on the right: the price level of the 235 metros whose polymer wage BLS publishes, US average = 100, BEA 2023. Akron sits at 92.9, the middle metro at 93.1.

Wage against price

The two things a recruiter is actually trading off

The further right a metro sits, the bigger the paycheck. The further up, the more that paycheck has to cover. So the top left is the worst trade a recruiter can make and the bottom right the best. Every metro on one dashed diagonal ends up with the same buying power, and the orange diagonal is Akron’s own: every metro above it buys less. Los Angeles is the sharpest case among the big employers, at a price level of 114.7 against Akron’s 92.9, so the same salary covers about a fifth less there.

What it means in an offer letter

Against Chicago, the price advantage is worth about $6,600 a year

Akron’s average polymer paycheck is $1,238 a week, which is $64,400 a year over 52 weeks. Chicago prices run 102.5 against Akron’s 92.9, so the same buying power costs a Chicago employer $1,365 a week, about $71,000 a year: $71,000 minus $64,400 is the $6,600 in the heading. San Francisco is dearer again at 117.6, where matching Akron takes about $1,566 a week, or $81,400. These are averages across every job the federal wage count covers in each metro, not any one person’s offer; the point is the size of the wedge a recruiter can put in a candidate’s spreadsheet.

A Chicago offer has to clear $71,000 to beat Akron’s $64,400.
