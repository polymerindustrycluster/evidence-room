Is the Tech Hub award big?

Routine federal contracts bring the region’s polymer industry about $37 million a year.

The Tech Hub award is $51.0 million: a competitive grant from the U.S. Economic Development Administration (EDA), the federal agency for regional development, to build up the region’s polymer industry across seven named projects. Routine federal contracting in chemical and plastics manufacturing across twelve Northeast Ohio counties has run at $36.6 million a year over the seven finished years, 2019 to 2025, so the award is worth about 1.4 years of what the region already gets.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster (PIC) desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data USAspending.gov and the PIC funding map · August 2026

The comparison

Set the award beside the money that was already coming

One bar per federal budget year, which runs October to September. Each counts obligations under the census industry codes for chemical (325) and plastics and rubber (326), for work in the twelve counties. In fiscal 2019 that routine flow, the government simply buying things, came to $51.6 million, $647,000 more than the whole award.

Obligation: money the government has legally committed to pay. It is a promise on the books, not cash already spent: the cash that actually goes out, the outlay, can follow years later. In 2025 dollars: older years marked up for inflation, so that a 2019 dollar and a 2025 dollar buy the same thing. As awarded is the smaller, unadjusted figure on the original paperwork. The award is the one unadjusted figure here: restated into 2025 dollars it is $51.3 million, still about 1.4 years, and fiscal 2019 clears it on that basis too.

$51.0M of Tech Hub money is about 1.4 years of what already arrives

Each bar is one year of that routine contracting, in 2025 dollars. The orange line is the Tech Hub award at the same scale, so a bar that passes it is a single year worth more than the whole award. The dashed line averages all eight bars, FY2026 included, so it runs below the $36.6 million a year the finished years give. Bar labels are rounded, so the two bars nearest the award line carry their exact distance from it.

Where the work is recorded. Place of performance is a reported field on the award, not an observation of where the work happened. A centrally administered contract, or a prime performing through a subaward, can put the county code somewhere other than the activity.

What it buys

The largest recipient is a category defined by what it is not

The same obligations, sorted by industry across all eight years and on the same 2025-dollar basis. Every business in federal data is filed under a six-digit industry number, the census code (NAICS), and the largest of them here, “all other rubber product manufacturing,” is the leftovers class for rubber goods that fit none of the named ones. It takes more of the eight-year total than any named industry does, and the award view in the next section opens the box: on that view, 99 cents in every dollar under this code belong to one company’s life rafts and escape suits.

Two rubber-product codes hold 65% of eight years of money

Federal obligations by six-digit industry code, FY2019 to FY2026 added together, in 2025 dollars. Each bar is one industry’s eight-year total, not a single year. Bar labels are shortened; the table carries the full census names.

The chart has two blind spots worth naming. University and research awards file under education and research industry codes, so two streams of real federal money in these counties appear in no bar above: the National Science Foundation’s NEO-SMART Engine, $15.0 million, and a second research program, TARDISS, whose size this page does not carry. And counting every industry in these twelve counties, rather than the polymer codes alone, gives $235.5 billion over the same years, nearly all of it aerospace, defense and health contracting with no polymer content. That is a count of something else, and this page never adds the two together. It does set the scale, though: polymer-coded work is about one dollar in every 840 of federal contracting here.

Concentration is the thing to watch in this chart. The two rubber codes hold 65% of the eight-year total. The second of them is tire manufacturing, which put $20.2 million into FY2022 and recorded nothing at all in FY2023. The region’s total fell $26.6 million between those two years, most of it from that one code, which is why FY2023 is the shortest bar in the year-by-year chart. Whether that is a year without tire contracting or a year without a recorded row, these files cannot say. Either way, a total this concentrated moves when one code goes quiet, which is the sense in which 65% is a risk and not only a fact.

That concentration also moves the yardstick the award is measured against. Take the leftovers code out of the seven finished years and the routine flow falls from $36.6 million a year to $25.0 million, which makes the award 2.0 years of what is left rather than 1.4; take tire manufacturing out as well and the flow is $13.7 million and the award is 3.7 years. Neither exclusion is the honest basis for the comparison, because both codes are federal money genuinely arriving in these counties, and each is very nearly one company, both named in the next section.

Who gets it

The leftovers class is almost entirely one company making escape gear

The industry chart can say what kind of company the money went to, and not which one. The contracts themselves carry names. Asked award by award instead of year by year, the same federal ledger returns 193 companies holding 6,630 prime contracts, the ones signed directly with the government. The buyer is mostly one: the Department of Defense placed 92 cents of every dollar. The largest holder is the survival-equipment maker RFD Beaufort, with $104 million for inflatable life rafts and submarine escape suits, bought through the Defense Logistics Agency, the Pentagon’s supply arm. Goodyear holds $79 million of military tires, some of them sold on to allied governments through the Foreign Military Sales program; the record names Israel. The field-equipment maker HDT Expeditionary Systems holds $39 million of field air conditioners that the census code files under plastics pipe and fittings.

Two companies hold 56% of the contract dollars

Each bar is one company’s total across every contract the FY2019–FY2026 window touches, counted in the dollars of the day: each year’s dollars as they were, with no adjustment for inflation. A total counts the award’s whole life, including years outside the window, which makes it a different measure from the yearly bars above, so bars here are never added to that chart.

The small end of the same ledger is busy. Below half a million dollars sit 6,557 awards, the middle one worth $1,500. Sherwin-Williams reaches its $23.4 million on 2,049 separate paint orders and Airgas its $16.0 million on 2,134 orders of industrial gas; one belting supplier, III Williams, appears 479 times for a combined $1.0 million of V-belts and seals. That is what routine means. The register below lists the big end instead: all 73 contracts of $500,000 or more, what each one bought, and a link to each award’s own page in the federal record.

The other kind of money

The award money has names on it

The register above names who sells and who buys. What it cannot do is say who holds any single year: an award’s total counts its whole life, so nothing on this page can split the $51.6 million recorded in fiscal 2019 among firms. The award money is traceable to the project instead. It went to seven leads, the organizations that each run one piece of the work: the largest is $11.1 million to BioVerde for bio-based butadiene, and the smallest is $2.7 million to stand up a regional office of innovation at the Greater Akron Chamber. The seven projects also carry $7.2 million in non-federal partner match (money the region and its firms put up alongside the award), which is not counted in the $51.0 million or anywhere else on this page.

Seven named awards make up the $51.0M line

EDA Tech Hub implementation awards by project lead, in the dollars each was awarded. The bar under each amount is drawn against the largest award, so a bar half as long is an award about half the size.

A number is big or small only against something.

The routine flow in these twelve counties runs about $36.6 million a year in 2025 dollars, counting the seven fiscal years that have closed. Fiscal 2026 is still running. Averaging it in with the seven closed years pulls the yearly figure down to $34.9 million, and a smaller yearly figure stretches the award further, to a year and a half. An annual rate should not carry a year that has not finished, so the comparison here uses closed years. The Tech Hub award, $51.0 million, is about 1.4 years of it, and in fiscal 2019 the routine flow was larger than the whole award on its own. A competitive grant and a routine purchase order are two different kinds of money, so what is compared here is rough size, not like for like. Whether $36.6 million a year is healthy or thin for a cluster this size is a different question, and these records (which count what was committed, not what was needed) cannot answer it.
