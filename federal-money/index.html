Is the Tech Hub award big?

Routine federal contracts bring the region’s polymer industry about $35 million a year.

The Tech Hub award is $51.0 million: a competitive grant from the U.S. Economic Development Administration (EDA), the federal agency for regional economic development, split across seven named local projects. Ordinary federal contracting in chemical and plastics manufacturing across the same twelve counties has run at $34.9 million a year since 2019, so the award is worth about a year and a half of money the region was already getting.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data USAspending.gov and the PIC funding map · August 2026

Obligation: money the government has legally committed to pay. It is a promise on the books, not cash already spent: the cash that actually goes out, the outlay, can follow years later. The obligation is the figure federal spending data reports. In 2025 dollars: older years marked up for inflation, so that a 2019 dollar and a 2025 dollar buy the same thing and the eight years can be compared. As awarded is the smaller, unadjusted figure on the original paperwork; the tables carry both. The award is shown as awarded, which is the one place this page puts an unadjusted figure beside adjusted ones: restated into 2025 dollars it is $51.3 million, and still about a year and a half.

Each bar is one year of ordinary federal contracting with the region’s polymer industry, in 2025 dollars. A bar that passes the orange line is a single year worth more than the whole Tech Hub award; the dashed line is the eight-year average. Where the work is recorded. Place of performance is a reported field on the award, not an observation of where the work happened. A centrally administered contract, or a prime performing through a subaward, can put the county code somewhere other than the activity.

What it buys

The largest recipient is a category defined by what it is not

The same obligations, sorted by industry across all eight years and on the same 2025-dollar basis. Every business in federal data is filed under a six-digit industry number, the census code (NAICS), and the one at the top of this chart, “all other rubber product manufacturing,” is the leftovers class for rubber goods that fit none of the named ones. It takes more of the eight years than any named industry does.

Two rubber-product codes hold 65% of eight years of money

Federal obligations by six-digit industry code, FY2019 to FY2026 added together, in 2025 dollars. Each bar is one industry’s eight-year total, not a single year. Bar labels are shortened; the table carries the full census names.

The chart has two blind spots worth naming. University and research awards file under education and research industry codes, so the National Science Foundation’s NEO-SMART Engine, $15.0 million, and TARDISS, another federally funded research program, are real federal money in these counties that appears in no bar above. And counting every industry in these twelve counties, rather than the polymer codes alone, gives $235.5 billion over the same years, nearly all of it aerospace, defense and health contracting with no polymer content. That is a count of something else and the two are never added together, but it does fix the scale: polymer-coded work is about one dollar in every 840 of federal contracting here.

Concentration is the thing to watch in this chart. The two rubber codes hold 65% of eight years, and the second of them, tire manufacturing, put $20.2 million into FY2022 and recorded nothing at all in FY2023: most of the $26.6 million the region’s total fell between those two years, and the reason FY2023 is the shortest bar in the year-by-year chart. Whether that is a year without tire contracting or a year without a recorded row, these files cannot say. Either way, a total this concentrated moves when one code goes quiet, which is the sense in which 65% is a risk and not only a fact.

The other kind of money

The award money has names on it

The routine flow is the government buying things, and it reaches this page as industry categories with no buyer and no seller attached, so nothing here can say which firms hold the $51.6 million recorded in fiscal 2019. The award reads down to the project. It went to seven leads, each with a named piece of work: $11.1 million to BioVerde for bio-based butadiene at the top, and $2.7 million at the bottom to stand up a regional office of innovation at the Greater Akron Chamber. The seven projects also carry $7.2 million in non-federal partner match (money the region and its firms put up alongside the award), which is not counted in the $51.0 million or anywhere else on this page.

Seven named awards make up the $51.0M line

EDA Tech Hub implementation awards by project lead, in the dollars each was awarded. The bar under each amount is drawn against the largest award, so a bar half as long is an award about half the size.

A number is big or small only against something.

Routine federal polymer contracting in these twelve counties runs about $34.9 million a year in 2025 dollars. The Tech Hub award, $51.0 million, is about 1.5 years of it, and in fiscal 2019 the routine flow was larger than the whole award on its own. A competitive grant and a routine purchase order are two different kinds of money, so what is compared here is rough size, not like for like. Whether $34.9 million a year is healthy or thin for a cluster this size is a different question, and these records (which count what was committed, not what was needed) cannot answer it.
