Where the people who work in each county live, 2022

Most people working in Summit County do not live there.

Every workforce claim PIC makes is scoped to a place — a county, a city, a footprint — and every one assumes the people counted in that place are from it. LEHD publishes the home county of every job in the country, so the assumption is checkable. Not one of the twelve counties reaches 69 percent, and Summit, the cluster's center, is at 48.4. That much is real, and on its own it would be misleading: measured against 397 counties in 73 peer metros, 95 percent of them are also below 69 percent and the PIC-12 counties sit slightly above the peer median. A county that does not contain its own workforce is what a metropolitan area IS. The finding is what happens when the unit changes: the county is not a labor market, and the twelve-county footprint very nearly is — 89.5 percent of the jobs here are held by people who live here, which among regions of comparable size is matched only by Pittsburgh.

The finding

Every county against every county

Each row is a county people work in; each column is a county they come home to. A self-contained labor market would be a bright diagonal and nothing else — that is the benchmark every cell is read against. The diagonal is the finding, and it is weak. The last column is the share held by people living outside the footprint entirely, so each row sums to one whole county's jobs.

The part that is not a commute

The imported workforce is two different things, and one of them never traveled

Twelve percent of jobs here are held by people living outside the footprint. Reported as one number it sounds like a commute shed. It is not: roughly half comes from metros two hours away, where nobody drives daily. A 2019 pull tests whether that is a pandemic effect — and it is not that either.

The reciprocal

Where the people who live here work is a different question

Every figure above is workplace-side: who fills the jobs that sit in a county. It says nothing about where that county's own residents work, which is a different population with a different denominator. Both directions are drawn here because publishing one invites a reader to assume the other — and they do not move together.

The benchmark

Ninety-five percent of metropolitan counties look like this

A county whose jobs are mostly held by outsiders is not a Northeast Ohio condition. It is close to the definition of a metropolitan area, which the federal government delineates using commuting flow. Measured the same way, 397 counties in 73 peer metros settle whether the pattern above is about this region or about the country.

What is actually distinctive

The county is not a labor market. The twelve-county region very nearly is.

Raise the unit from the county to the footprint and the picture inverts. Among regions of comparable size, only Pittsburgh holds as much of its own workforce inside its own borders. Bigger regions contain more by construction, so this is drawn against region size rather than as a ranking.

The footprint is not an administrative convenience. It is the smallest honest unit.
