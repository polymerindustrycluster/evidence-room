Who fills each county’s jobs, 2022

Most people working in Summit County do not live there.

Census payroll records name the home county of every job in America, and they put Summit County’s own residents at 48.4 percent of its 265,460 jobs. Before that number can mean anything, it has to survive one comparison: the same measure, taken everywhere else.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data U.S. Census LEHD LODES, 2019 and 2022 · August 2026

The finding

No county fills even seven of ten jobs with its own residents

The diagonal alone

How much of each county’s workforce is its own

The diagonal of the matrix, pulled out and ranked. The line at half is not an official standard. It is the point where a county stops filling most of its own jobs with people who live there, the quiet assumption behind most single-county workforce claims.

Each bar is one county’s 2022 jobs. The longer the bar, the more of that work goes to people who live in the county. Orange: under half.

The part that is not a commute

The imported workforce is two different things, and one of them never traveled

Reported as one number, that share sounds like a ring of people driving in every morning. Roughly half of it comes from metros two hours away, where nobody drives daily. A 2019 pull tests whether remote work created the pattern. It did not.

One paycheck

About 27,000 of these jobs go home to Columbus

Franklin County alone accounts for 26,960 of the jobs worked inside the twelve counties, up from 23,506 in 2019. Picture one: a quality engineer whose paycheck says Barberton and whose mortgage says Columbus, on site for launch weeks and remote the rest. LODES counts her job in Summit County and her home two hours south, and records no trip between them. She is illustrative, a composite consistent with the data rather than a person in it; the counts are real.

The other direction

Where the people who live here work is a different question

Everything above is workplace-side: who fills the jobs that sit in a county. Where that county’s own residents work is a different group of people, counted against a different total. Both directions are drawn here because publishing one invites the reader to assume the other, and the two do not move together.

Each county gets two dots. The filled dot: of the jobs sitting in the county, how many go to its own residents. The open dot: of the jobs its residents hold, how many sit in the county. 2022.

Filled: jobs here held by residents here Open: residents’ jobs that sit in their own county

The benchmark

Ninety-five percent of metropolitan counties look like this

A county whose jobs are mostly held by outsiders is close to the definition of a metropolitan area, which the federal government draws from commuting flow. So the fair test is other metros. Measured identically, 397 counties in 73 peer metros settle whether the pattern above is about this region or about the country.

The twelve counties sit inside the national pack, a little above its middle

What is actually distinctive

The twelve-county region very nearly is a labor market

Raise the unit from the county to the twelve counties together and the picture inverts. Among regions of comparable size, only Pittsburgh holds more of its own workforce inside its own borders, and it does that on eight counties rather than twelve. A region with more counties keeps more work inside itself for arithmetic reasons alone, so the bars are ordered by how many counties each region has.

Each bar is one region. The longer the bar, the more of the work inside it goes to people who also live inside it. Ordered by county count, largest first; every single-state peer metro of six or more counties. Every bar counts only the jobs held by residents of the region’s own state, which is a narrower basis than the charts above use and gives the twelve counties a smaller job total.

Twelve counties is the smallest unit that holds this labor market whole.
