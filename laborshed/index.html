Where the people who work in each county live, 2022

Most people working in Summit County do not live there.

Census payroll records name the home county of every job in America, and they put Summit County’s own residents at 48.4 percent of its 265,460 jobs. Before that number can mean anything, it has to survive one comparison: the same measure, taken everywhere else.

The finding

No county fills even seven of ten jobs with its own residents

Each row is a county people work in; each column is a county they come home to. The data are LODES, a Census file that records, for every job in the country, the worker’s home county and the job’s county. A self-contained labor market would light up the diagonal and nothing else. The last column is the share held by people living outside the footprint entirely, so each row adds up to one whole county’s jobs.

The diagonal alone

How much of each county’s workforce is its own

The diagonal of the matrix, pulled out and ranked. The line at half is not an official standard. It is the point where a county stops being a majority-resident workplace, the quiet assumption behind most single-county workforce claims.

The part that is not a commute

The imported workforce is two different things, and one of them never traveled

Twelve percent of the jobs here are held by people living outside the footprint. Reported as one number, that sounds like a commute shed. Roughly half of it comes from metros two hours away, where nobody drives daily. A 2019 pull tests whether remote work created that pattern. It did not.

Jobs located in the twelve counties and held by people living outside them, 2019 and 2022.

One paycheck

About 27,000 of these jobs go home to Columbus

Franklin County alone accounts for 26,960 of the jobs worked inside the footprint, up from 23,506 in 2019. Picture one: a quality engineer whose paycheck says Barberton and whose mortgage says Columbus, on site for launch weeks and remote the rest. LODES counts her job in Summit County and her home two hours south, and records no trip between them. She is illustrative, a composite consistent with the data rather than a person in it; the counts are real.

The reciprocal

Where the people who live here work is a different question

Everything above is workplace-side: who fills the jobs that sit in a county. Where that county’s own residents work is a separate population with a separate denominator. Both directions are drawn here because publishing one invites the reader to assume the other, and the two do not move together.

The benchmark

Ninety-five percent of metropolitan counties look like this

A county whose jobs are mostly held by outsiders is close to the definition of a metropolitan area, which the federal government draws from commuting flow. So the fair test is other metros. Measured identically, 397 counties in 73 peer metros settle whether the pattern above is about this region or about the country.

What is actually distinctive

The county is not a labor market. The twelve-county region very nearly is.

Raise the unit from the county to the footprint and the picture inverts. Among regions of comparable size, only Pittsburgh holds as much of its own workforce inside its own borders. Bigger regions contain more by construction, so this is drawn against region size rather than as a ranking.

Share of 2022 jobs held by residents of the same region; every single-state peer metro of six or more counties.

The footprint is not an administrative convenience. It is the smallest honest unit.
