Who fills each county’s jobs, 2022

No county holds this labor market alone. All twelve do.

Census payroll records name the home county of every job in America, and they put Summit County’s own residents at 48.4 percent of its 265,460 jobs. Before that number can mean anything, it has to survive one comparison: the same measure, taken everywhere else.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data U.S. Census LEHD LODES, 2019 and 2022 · August 2026

The finding

No county fills even seven of ten jobs with its own residents

Each county alone

How much of each county’s workforce is its own

One number for each county, pulled from the chart above and ranked: the share of its jobs held by its own residents. The chart below marks half, not as an official standard but as the point where a county stops filling most of its own jobs with people who live there, the quiet assumption behind most single-county workforce claims.

Each of the twelve bars is one county’s 2022 jobs. The longer the bar, the more of that work goes to people who live in the county. Orange: under half.

The part that is not a commute

The imported workforce is two different things, and one of them never traveled

The imported workforce is every job inside the twelve counties held by someone who lives outside them. Reported as one number, it sounds like a ring of people driving in every morning. The chart below splits it by home county into adjacent counties, close enough for a daily drive; distant Ohio metros, roughly two hours away, where nobody drives daily; and everywhere else, counties too scattered to group. Within the two named groups, roughly half the jobs come from the distant metros. Against the whole imported workforce the distant metros come to 29 percent, because the largest single piece of the whole is the scattered everywhere-else group. The same payroll records for 2019 test whether remote work created the pattern; they show it was already there before the pandemic.

One paycheck

About 27,000 of these jobs go home to Columbus

Franklin County, home of Columbus, alone accounts for 26,960 of the jobs worked inside the twelve counties, Summit and its eleven neighbors, up from 23,506 in 2019. Picture one: a quality engineer whose paycheck says Barberton, in Summit County, and whose mortgage says Columbus, on site for launch weeks and remote the rest. LODES, the Census file behind every chart here, counts her job in Summit County and her home two hours south, and records no trip between them. She is illustrative, a composite consistent with the data rather than a person in it; the counts are real.

The other direction

Where the people who live here work is a different question

Everything above is workplace-side: who fills the jobs that sit in a county. Where that county’s own residents work is a different group of people, counted against a different total. Both directions are drawn here because publishing one invites the reader to assume the other, and the two do not move together.

Each county gets two dots. The filled dot: of the jobs sitting in the county, how many go to its own residents. The open dot: of the jobs its residents hold, how many sit in the county. 2022.

The benchmark

Ninety-five percent of metropolitan counties look like this

A county whose jobs are mostly held by outsiders is close to the definition of a metropolitan area, which the federal government draws from commuting flow. So the fair test is other metros. The peers are the 73 metros that sit inside one state and are big enough to clear a minimum job count. Measured identically, their 397 counties settle whether the pattern above is about this region or about the country.

On the share of jobs held by own residents, the twelve counties sit inside the national pack, a little above its middle

What is actually distinctive

The twelve-county region very nearly is a labor market

Raise the unit from the county to the twelve counties together and the picture inverts. Among regions of comparable size, only Pittsburgh keeps more of its work inside itself, and it does that on eight counties rather than twelve. A region with more counties keeps more work inside itself for arithmetic reasons alone, so the bars are ordered by how many counties each region has.

Each bar is one region. The longer the bar, the more of the work inside it goes to people who also live inside it. Shown: every peer metro of six or more counties, ordered by county count, largest first. As on the benchmark above, the source files count only the jobs held by residents of the region’s own state. The charts earlier on the page count residents of any state, which is why the twelve counties carry a smaller job total here.

Twelve counties hold this labor market nearly whole. No one of them comes close alone.
