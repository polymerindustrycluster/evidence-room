PIC’s own scorecard · measurement coverage, August 2026

Public records fill eight of the fifteen rows PIC answers for, and none of the four on revenue.

The Polymer Industry Cluster (PIC), an initiative of the Greater Akron Chamber, has won $85.3 million in public grant money, and almost all of it already has a named recipient. The award register shows $79.2 million of it on an executed line, which is a signed agreement naming the organisation that will receive the money. The register records no payment against any of it. The four rows that would tell a board whether PIC can pay for itself are measured in PIC’s membership register and general ledger, and this public repository carries neither.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data from the PIC award register, IPEDS and BLS · August 2026

One square is one metric. Filled squares are the rows a public record can fill, outlines are the rows whose numbers live only in PIC’s own registers, and the grey run at the end is cluster context PIC does not control.

Read this first

This is an internal working view, built only from public federal and state data. It is not a published account of PIC’s finances or membership.

This repository is public and its history is permanent, so it carries no member, applicant or personal data. Seven rows below are therefore unpopulated on purpose: their measurement lives in PIC’s membership register, general ledger and drawdown records. Each one is published here with its definition, cadence and target field ready, and with a visible empty state instead of a figure. Filling them needs a copy of this page kept outside a public repository, and nothing on this page should be read as an estimate of what those cells would say.

The scorecard

Seven of the fifteen rows PIC answers for are blank

One row per metric, in the four groups a board reads in order. Groups A, B and C are what PIC is accountable for: whether it can pay for itself, whether the award money it won reaches the organisations it was won for, and whether the region trains the graduates the cluster hires. Group D is the regional economy PIC works on and does not control, kept at the bottom, set apart, and never averaged into anything above it.

Eight rows compute from public records, seven wait on PIC’s own registers, three are context

A blank cell reads “not published here” and never reads zero. The coloured line at each row’s left edge repeats that status, so it does not rest on the cell alone.

What the target and owner columns mean. No target here was set by PIC. A target showing a figure is a ceiling fixed by a signed award document; every other target reads “not set”, and that absence is the finding rather than a gap in the build. No owner cell names a person, because this repository holds no owner assignment for any row, and under the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS), the management framework this scorecard follows, a row without a named owner is not yet a scorecard row.

The groups exist to hold one distinction. The scorecard measures what PIC itself is accountable for. Cluster health measures the regional polymer economy, which PIC influences through award money, convening and programs but does not control. The two must never be averaged or scored on one line. Letting organisational growth stand in for regional outcomes, or regional growth stand in for organisational performance, is the standard way economic-development reporting goes wrong: the cluster adds jobs for reasons no association caused, and the association reports them as delivery.

Group B · award delivery

Every award dollar without a named recipient sits inside one state grant

The register carries three public awards. Two of them are fully assigned, meaning every dollar names the organisation that receives it. The award from the federal Economic Development Administration (EDA) is split into seven implementation awards, each running straight to the project lead that will spend it, and the APEX award, the region’s Good Jobs Challenge workforce grant, is assigned in a single line. Everything still unassigned is Ohio money, held in two workstreams whose sub-grants have not been signed yet.

$6.2M of the $85.3M awarded has no named recipient yet, and all of it is Ohio money

One bar per public award, drawn to the same dollar scale. The solid part is money already assigned, with the organisation that receives it named on a signed line. The hatched part is money awarded to PIC with no recipient named yet. Each figure is rounded to the nearest tenth of a million dollars, so the two parts do not add exactly to the total.

One quantity is missing from that chart and cannot be drawn from any public source: how much of the money has actually been paid. An obligation is a legal commitment to pay, and an executed sub-grant is a signed agreement to pay. Neither is a payment. The disbursement row in group B, the money actually paid out, is empty for that reason. It is the row a board should ask about first, because a fully assigned award that has disbursed nothing and a fully assigned award that has disbursed everything look identical on this page.

Group C · talent

Polymer credentials fell by more than half in 2022 and have not come back

Federal education data covers the degree side of the talent question: how many people finish a polymer credential each year at the three regional universities that confer them, Case Western Reserve, Kent State and the University of Akron. It says nothing about PIC’s own workforce programs, which are the two empty rows in group C. Materials credentials at those same universities rose across the same three years, 2021 to 2023, from 20 to 33, while polymer credentials were falling.

Polymer credentials ran near 120 a year for a decade, then dropped to 54

Credentials at every level, at those same three universities, in every polymer field of study the federal Classification of Instructional Programs (CIP) recognises, by federal reporting year. Higher is more graduates. The series skips 2020, because the federal data mirror republished the 2019 counts under that year. Three readings come off this series: 54 is the 2022 low, 63 is the latest year, and the 80 in the scorecard row is the average of 2021, 2022 and 2023.

The objection

Why a blank cell is worth more than a close-enough one

The obvious complaint about this page is that seven blanks make a poor board document, and that a rounded band or a rough share would at least give the room something to talk about.

The answer is that a scorecard is read as a measurement, and a number in a scorecard cell is treated as one for years after whoever typed it has forgotten where it came from. Once a plausible membership figure sits in this table, nobody re-derives it; they compare against it. The seven blanks are therefore doing the work: each one names the register that holds the real number, the definition that number has to satisfy, and the cadence it has to arrive on, so that populating it later is a lookup rather than a judgment call. The cost of the blank is one awkward board meeting. The cost of a plausible wrong number is a year of decisions taken against it.

Public records answer where PIC’s award money was assigned and say nothing about whether PIC can pay for itself.

Eight of the fifteen accountable rows compute from federal and state records: $79.2 million of $85.3 million awarded carries a named recipient, and 63 polymer credentials were awarded in the region in 2023 against a three-year average of 80. Seven rows, including all four on revenue and the one on disbursement, are defined and empty. Three more are cluster context that belongs to the region rather than to PIC, and they stay in their own group for that reason.
