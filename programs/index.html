Polymer Industry Cluster Evidence room · polymer education

Every polymer degree and certificate in the federal record, 1991–2023

Northeast Ohio wants to rebuild its polymer technician pipeline. Of the three layers of polymer education, it is the only one that did not hold.

Polymer education has three layers: degrees, technician programs and certificates, and the adjacent trades. Of the 73 polymer technician programs that ever took hold, just 20 were still awarding a credential in 2023, a little over one in four. Welding and machining, taught in the same buildings by the same colleges, came through at nearly twice that rate, and so did polymer degrees.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data IPEDS completions by 6-digit CIP, The same IPEDS pull for six peer technician CI and 1 more · August 2026

The finding

Two of these three layers held. The technician layer lost nearly three programs in four

The three layers are the polymer technician programs, the polymer degrees above them, and the peer trades taught next door. One rule measures all three over the same years. A program is in this chart if it took hold, meaning it ever conferred more than ten completions, and it counts as surviving if it conferred one in 2023. Six peer trades are here for one reason. Welding, machining, industrial maintenance, manufacturing engineering technology, electrical engineering technology and mechanical engineering technology are taught in the same buildings by the same kind of college, so if they had fallen as far, this would be a story about community colleges rather than about polymers. They did not. The trades and the polymer degrees finish within a point of each other; the technician layer finishes twenty points below both. One limit belongs with that finding. The polymer codes are the federal catalogue numbers a college puts on a program when it reports completions, and this record sees only what colleges chose to file under them. No one has rebuilt this layer in the paperwork. Whether anyone has done it on the ground is a question the paperwork cannot answer.

The record

After a collapse and a recovery, completions are a third below their 2016 peak

Every polymer completion in the record, added up by year, certificates through doctorates. It fell through the 2000s, then more than doubled from the 2007 floor to the 2016 peak. Since that peak, completions have fallen in four of the five years that can be compared with the year before them. The gap in that comparison is 2020: the federal mirror republished 2019’s counts under that year, so this record has no real 2020 to measure against.

The base rate

More than half of the technician programs that ever existed never took hold

The chart above counts only the programs that took hold. This one drops that filter and looks at every technician program in the record, all 168 of them, because the ones that never took hold are the decision-relevant fact. Programs kept being started, and more than half, 95 of the 168, never took hold. Anyone proposing to rebuild this layer is proposing to beat that base rate, and should know it before, not after.

What working looks like

Sixteen technician programs ever passed a hundred completions, and one of them is not like the others

Each bar is one program’s lifetime completions, for the sixteen that ever passed one hundred. Dark bars are still conferring; light bars ended. Ferris State, a public university in Michigan, is the outlier to study: more completions than the next two programs combined, across 33 unbroken years. Why it held while the layer around it thinned is a question this dataset cannot answer. It is the first question to put to the people who run the program.

The anchor

In 2023 the University of Akron, the world’s flagship polymer school, graduated two polymer undergraduates

The University of Akron’s polymer completions, every year the record covers. The collapse is recent and steep, and most of the loss sits at the top, in the master’s and doctoral degrees. It ran through the same years in which federal and state grant money for polymer work arrived in the region. Akron is this system’s anchor, the institution the rest of it is built around, and a technician pipeline built on top of an anchor that is contracting is a different proposition from one built on an anchor that is holding.

What it pays

Where the record shows earnings at all, they are strong: $87k–$101k four years out

The College Scorecard, the federal database of what graduates of a given program go on to earn, publishes earnings for polymer engineering bachelor’s degrees at exactly five institutions. None is in Ohio. They are Penn College, Penn State Behrend, UMass Lowell, Western Washington and the University of Wisconsin-Stout (UW-Stout). Each row shows median earnings one year and four years after graduation. The pale band is the national comparison: the middle half of all programs filed under the same federal code, four years out.

The turn

No one has rebuilt one

This dataset contains no verified example of a substantial polymer technician program dying and being rebuilt. One case looked like a rebuild. It turned out to be a federal reporting change: the program never stopped teaching, and a renumbering split its record into an ending and a fresh start. A region proposing to rebuild this layer is proposing to do something this record offers no example of anyone doing.

What “rebuild” means depends on which fact you take as the problem: the survival rate, the coin-flip base rate of new starts, or the contracting anchor. Each points at a different program design, and the record is compatible with all three readings, which is exactly why the design choice has to be made on purpose.

There is no verified rebuild case in this record. Whoever builds the next polymer technician program is writing the first one: against a coin-flip base rate, on top of a contracting anchor, in the layer that survived worst.
