Polymer Industry Cluster Evidence room · polymer education

Every polymer degree and certificate in the federal record, 1991–2023

Northeast Ohio wants to rebuild its polymer technician pipeline. Of the three layers of polymer education, it is the only one that did not hold.

Barely one in four polymer technician programs that ever got going was still awarding a credential in 2023. Welding and machining, taught in the same buildings by the same colleges, came through at nearly twice that rate, and so did polymer degrees.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data IPEDS completions by 6-digit CIP, The same IPEDS pull for six peer technician CI and 1 more · August 2026

The finding

Two of these three layers held. The technician layer lost three programs in four

One rule measures all three layers over the same years. A program is in this chart if it ever conferred more than ten completions, and it counts as surviving if it conferred one in 2023. The six peer trades are here for one reason: welding, machining, industrial maintenance and their neighbours are taught in the same buildings by the same kind of college, so if they had fallen as far, this would be a story about community colleges rather than about polymers. They did not. The trades and the polymer degrees finish within a point of each other; the technician layer finishes twenty points below both. One limit travels with the headline above and belongs down here rather than up there: this record sees only what colleges chose to file under the polymer codes, so no one has rebuilt this layer in the paperwork, which is not the same as no one having done it on the ground.

The record

After a collapse and a recovery, completions are a third below their 2016 peak

Every polymer completion in the record, added up by year, certificates through doctorates. It fell through the 2000s, more than doubled from the 2007 floor to the 2016 peak, and has declined in four of the five years since that can be compared. The line breaks at 2020, a year this record cannot see.

The base rate

Most technician programs that ever existed never took hold

The chart above counts programs that got past ten completions. This one drops that filter and looks at all 168, because the ones that never got going are the decision-relevant fact. Programs kept being started, and about half never took hold. Anyone proposing to rebuild this layer is proposing to beat that base rate, and should know it before, not after.

What working looks like

Sixteen technician programs ever passed a hundred completions, and one of them is not like the others

Each bar is one program’s lifetime completions, for the sixteen that ever passed one hundred. Dark bars are still conferring; light bars ended. Ferris State is the outlier to study: more completions than the next two programs combined, across 33 unbroken years. Why it held while the layer around it thinned is a question this dataset cannot answer, and it is the first interview to do.

The anchor

In 2023 the University of Akron, the world’s flagship polymer school, graduated two polymer undergraduates

Akron’s polymer completions, every year the record covers. The collapse is recent, steep, and top-heavy, and it ran through the same years the region’s federal and state polymer awards arrived. A technician pipeline built on top of a contracting anchor is a different proposition from one built on a stable anchor.

What it pays

Where the record shows earnings at all, they are strong: $87k–$101k four years out

The federal Scorecard publishes earnings for polymer engineering bachelor’s degrees at exactly five institutions, and none is in Ohio. For each: median earnings one year and four years after graduation, with the national middle half of the program code behind them.

The turn

No one has rebuilt one

This dataset contains no verified example of a substantial polymer technician program dying and being rebuilt. The one candidate dissolved on inspection into a federal reporting change at a program that never stopped teaching. A region proposing to rebuild this layer is proposing to do something this record offers no example of anyone doing.

What “rebuild” means depends on which fact you take as the problem: the survival rate, the coin-flip base rate of new starts, or the contracting anchor. Each points at a different program design, and the record is compatible with all three readings, which is exactly why the design choice has to be made on purpose.

There is no verified rebuild case in this record. Whoever builds the next polymer technician program is writing the first one: against a coin-flip base rate, on top of a contracting anchor, in the layer that survived worst.
