Polymer Industry Cluster Method · sources, codes and the traps

A replication guide for other regions

You can rebuild this for your own region. The codes are the hard part, and they are all on this page.

Five worked questions run the whole way here, from the endpoint, the web address the data comes from, to the reading, the number you end up printing. They use the public sources behind these analyses. The first one needs a browser, a spreadsheet and two file downloads: no key, no registration, and nothing from this repository. What costs you time is not the data, it is deciding which codes count as your industry, so that decision is worked through rather than assumed. Also here: all twenty-seven of the site’s sources, what each one cannot tell you, and the traps that give a confidently wrong answer rather than an obviously wrong one.

The data estate

Twenty-seven sources, one row each

The worked question

Is your region unusually concentrated in an industry?

The measure is the location quotient: the share of local jobs an industry fills, divided by the share it fills nationally. It is what most regional strategies open with, it runs on the most accessible of the twenty-seven sources, and it exercises every trap worth learning at once: which codes count as your industry, what happens when you add a code family to its own parts, what a blank cell means, and what the answer does not tell you. Seven steps, worked here on the twelve Northeast Ohio counties this site covers. Nothing below needs anything from this repository.

  1. Choose your codes, and know that you are choosing

    There is no polymer line in federal industry statistics, and there is very likely no line for your industry either. Somebody picks a set of North American Industry Classification System codes and that set decides every number that follows. This site counts three: 3252 resins, synthetic rubber and fibres, 3255 paints, coatings and adhesives, and 326 plastics and rubber products. The concentration page reads six, adding the wider chemical family 325 and the two halves of 326, 3261 plastics products and 3262 rubber products.

    Never add a family to its own parts. 326 contains 3261 and 3262; 325 contains 3252 and 3255. Summing a column that holds both is the first mistake, it is invisible in the output, and the next section draws exactly what it costs here.

  2. Pull the data

    The Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages publishes open comma-separated slices, one file per area per period, with no key and no registration.

    https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/{year}/a/area/{fips}.csv

    The {fips} is the five-digit county code, and the a is the annual average. Take annual averages rather than a quarter, because these series are not seasonally adjusted and adjacent quarters are not comparable. One area file already holds every industry level for that area, so the all-industry total you will divide by is the row carrying industry code 10 in the file you just downloaded. Download one more file, for area US000, and it holds the national pair. The employment count you divide is the column named annual_avg_emplvl. Do not reach for lq_annual_avg_emplvl beside it: that is the bureau’s own finished location quotient, on its own basis, and step six is about why the two differ.

    The aggregation level is the county filter, and it is not the state one. In plain words: these are the bureau’s own ownership and aggregation codes, and each one says what a row is a total of, whether a whole county, one kind of employer, or one width of industry code. Every row carries an agglvl_code saying which rung of the geography-by-industry ladder it sits on, and the rungs are numbered separately per geography. In a county file, 70 is the county total across all ownerships, 71 the county total by ownership, 74 the sector, 75 a three-digit NAICS code, 76 four-digit, 77 five-digit and 78 six-digit. So 326 is 75 and 3252 is 76. The US000 file numbers the same ladder from 10 rather than 70, and it is not a rung-for-rung translation of the county list. The ones this recipe needs there are 10, 11, 15, 16 and 18. The 50-to-58 block belongs to STATE files and appears in no county file at all, and it has four rungs of its own: 55 three-digit, 56 four-digit, 57 five-digit and 58 six-digit.

    This page told you the wrong ones until 2026-08-29. It said to filter to 55, 57 and 58 for county by industry. Those are the state 3-, 5- and 6-digit levels. They return zero rows from the county file this same step tells you to download, and a reader who applied them got an empty table on a page whose own trap section says never to let a filter that returns nothing read as a finding of zero. The codes were real: they belong to one state-level pull elsewhere in this site’s register, and the recipe borrowed them without reading which geography they were for.

    Ownership is not a choice here, and this is where the arithmetic goes. Filter to own_code 5, private ownership: in these files the industry rows exist at own_code 5 and nowhere else, so there is no all-ownership row for 326 to pick even if you wanted one. The all-industry denominator is the opposite: take own_code 0, the total across every ownership. That pairing is deliberate and the next step is about why.

  3. Do the arithmetic

    A location quotient is a ratio of two shares. It asks whether your industry fills more of your local job market than it fills of the country’s.

    LQ = (local industry jobs, private ÷ local jobs, all industries, all ownerships) ÷ (national industry jobs, private ÷ national jobs, all industries, all ownerships)

    Four numbers, all four in the two files you just pulled. Nothing is modelled, nothing is estimated, and there is no confidence interval, because the source is a census of insured employment rather than a survey.

    The top and the bottom are on different ownerships on purpose, and that will look like a mistake. The intuitive rule is to hold ownership constant across all four numbers, and this page taught that rule until 2026-08-29. It is a coherent measure and it is not the one the bureau publishes. Held constant at private, the worked cell below comes out 3.12 rather than 3.27, 4.5% lower. BLS divides private industry employment by all-ownership totals, and the number every published location quotient on this site sits on is that one. Do not quietly switch the rule to whichever produces your figure: pick the bureau’s if you want to be comparable to anybody else’s, and say which you used either way.

    The worked cell, both ways. Summit County, 326, 2025. BLS’s basis is (3,824 ÷ 259,944) ÷ (700,170 ÷ 155,685,781) = 3.271, which rounds to the 3.27 the file prints and this page publishes. Holding ownership at private throughout instead gives (3,824 ÷ 232,800) ÷ (700,170 ÷ 133,117,983) = 3.123. Same four files, same codes, different measure. Read on 2026-08-29: local: own_code 5 industry 326 agglvl 75; own_code 0 industry 10 agglvl 70; own_code 5 industry 10 agglvl 71. national (US000): own_code 5 industry 326 agglvl 15; own_code 0 industry 10 agglvl 10; own_code 5 industry 10 agglvl 11.

    The file proves its own denominator, and this is the check to run first. Those area files carry a location-quotient column of their own, lq_annual_avg_emplvl. Look at the rows for industry 10 at each own_code: on a same-ownership basis each of those would be exactly 1.00, because it would be dividing a total by itself. In this file they read 1.05 for private, 0.88 for local government, 0.44 for state and 0.39 for federal. That is the bureau telling you, in its own file, what its denominator is.

    Which of the two this page prints, and how that was settled. The 3.27 above is BLS’s own lq_annual_avg_emplvl column, carried through this site’s pay page unchanged. That is not an assumption: the concentration page computes its own value from components and ships BLS’s beside it, the two round apart on 2 of 601 cells, and the printed figure follows the bureau’s on 601 of 601 and ours on 599. Our independent recomputation of this cell is 3.271, 0.00102 away, and across 667 checked cells the two never differ by more than 0.004997, which is half of the last digit the bureau prints. So: the number is the bureau’s, we can reproduce it, and it is on the bureau’s mixed-ownership basis rather than the same-basis rule this page used to teach.

  4. Read it against 1.0, and only against 1.0

    A reading of 1.0 means the industry fills exactly the same share of local jobs as it does of the country’s. Above 1.0 the region is more concentrated than the country, below it, less. This site’s worked case, drawn at the top of this page: Summit County, Ohio, which is area 39153 in that endpoint, reads 3.27 for plastics and rubber, NAICS 326, in 2025, on 3,824 jobs. The measure is a share of a share, so it has no unit and cannot be added, averaged across geographies, or compared between two different industry definitions.

  5. Two rules that will bite you

    A withheld cell is not a zero. Statistical agencies suppress any cell where too few employers would be identifiable, and the suppressed cells are disproportionately the small ones. Treating a blank as a zero gives a confidently wrong answer. There is a section on this below, with the rates.

    A high reading on a small base is fragile. Concentration is a share, not a size. A single plant closing can halve it. Print the job count beside every reading so nobody can quote the ratio without the base, which is why the chart at the top of this page carries both. And print what high means here. Of the 1,732 county-by-four-digit private cells the bureau discloses across these twelve counties in 2025, the 22.7 is the fifth highest: three primary-metal cells in Mahoning and Trumbull and one basic-chemical cell in Ashtabula read higher. A reading can be the largest a page publishes and still be ordinary for the place, and without a reference distribution a reader has no way to tell which.

    The reference distribution is a dated derived snapshot, read 1 September 2026, preserved from the published version. Its raw county responses are not included; rebuilding this guide preserves the snapshot and checks its subject and year, rather than re-fetching the full comparison universe.

  6. If your region is more than one county, you have to build it yourself

    No statistical agency publishes a location quotient for a custom geography, so a region-wide reading is yours and not the bureau’s, and it has to be built from components rather than averaged from county readings. A location quotient cannot be averaged across places. Sum the industry jobs over your counties, sum the all-industry totals over the same counties, divide one by the other, and divide that by the national share you already have from the US000 file.

    Withheld counties are the hard part, and they behave differently for the two kinds of number. A withheld county drops out of the numerator and the denominator together, so it does not drag the ratio toward zero, but it does not leave it untouched either. Job and site counts built this way are floors: the missing counties’ jobs are simply absent. The ratio is neither a floor nor a census. You cannot sign the direction of that error, and it is tempting to try. Withheld counties are disproportionately the small ones, so it reads like a ceiling: drop the small places and the ratio should rise. But small does not mean unconcentrated. The most concentrated cell this site publishes is also one of its smallest, at 22.7 times the national share on 491 jobs, and the smallest cells it publishes run from 0.09 to 22.7. Losing one of those pulls the regional ratio down, not up. Report the ratio with the count of counties inside it, every time, and say the direction is unknown rather than guessing it, because that set moves between years.

    The county aggregate used for the wage analysis is PIC-12, and these are the twelve counties, named because a reader replicating this needs the set and not its size, each with the five-digit FIPS code the filter actually takes: Ashtabula 39007, Cuyahoga 39035, Geauga 39055, Lake 39085, Lorain 39093, Mahoning 39099, Medina 39103, Portage 39133, Stark 39151, Summit 39153, Trumbull 39155, Wayne 39169. The chain page uses these twelve counties plus Columbiana and Tuscarawas. Metro areas, Ohio totals and national comparisons have their own boundaries; compare figures only after matching their county sets.

  7. What it lands on here

    The same pull, filtered the same way, produces the concentration page and, with wages instead of employment counts, the pay-premium page. That is the point worth taking away: one file, filtered once, answers a concentration question and a pay question and a peer-ranking question, and most of the work in all three is deciding what counts as your industry.

The second worked question

Does that industry pay better than the rest of your regional economy?

This one is nearly free once you have the first: the same file, the same filters, one column across. What it costs instead is a decision about what you divide by, and then a trap that has nothing to do with the data and everything to do with the sentence you write over it. Four steps. This site got the sentence wrong, and the wrong version is the one a replicator writes first.

  1. Take the wage column, not the employment one

    The county file you already downloaded carries annual_avg_wkly_wage beside the employment column. No second request, no different endpoint. Everything you decided in the first recipe still holds: the same code set, the same ownership, the same aggregation level, the same annual average.

    An average weekly wage is not a pay rate. It moves with hours worked and with the mix of occupations inside the industry, not only with what an employer pays for a given job. A region whose plants run more overtime reads as higher paying. State this or your reader will hear a rate.

  2. Decide what you are dividing by, because there are two questions here

    A wage on its own says nothing, so it needs a denominator, and the two obvious ones answer different questions. Divide by the same industry nationally and you are asking whether your region is a good place to hold this job. Divide by the average job in the same county and you are asking whether the industry pays above its own town. Those two can point opposite ways at once, and here they do.

    This site publishes both, for every cell. Against the same industry nationally, 44 of the 51 published cells pay less than the industry pays elsewhere, a middle cell of 0.88×. Against the average job in the same county, 40 of the 51 pay more. The same cells, the same year, opposite verdicts, and both are true.

    Neither is the right one. They are different questions, and a page that prints one while its headline asks the other is the most common way this recipe goes wrong before the arithmetic even starts.

  3. The trap: what your median is a median of

    Now you have a ratio for every industry-and-county cell your agency will publish, and you want one number. You take the median. The question nobody asks out loud is what that median runs over, and there are at least three answers sitting in the same table.

    The table holds 51 published cells out of 72 possible, six industry codes across twelve counties. Take the middle of those 51 and the unit is a cell. Count each county once and the unit is a county-industry. The rule is to keep the finest detail published for that county and drop the family above it: where a county publishes 3252 or 3255, drop its 325 row; where it publishes 3261 or 3262, drop its 326. That leaves 35 rows, of which 27 pay above their county. Weight the group-average ratios by employment; a coarser set can carry that: the two industry groups taken once per county, 33,528 jobs, 29,080 of them in a group that out-pays its county.

    median of 51 cell-average ratios = 1.21×  ·  employment-weighted median of group-average ratios = 1.26×

    Read the weighted group figure with its boundary attached. The only complete non-overlapping set the published data offer is NAICS 325 and 326 taken once per county, so that is what a job weighting can run over at all. It is wider than this site’s own measurement register of 3252, 3255, 326, which covers 24,030 jobs: 15,758 of the 33,528 weighted jobs, about 47 percent, are chemistry the register calls context rather than cluster. The finer set cannot be weighted because it is not published for every county. That is a real limit, not a rounding note. Neither statistic reveals the distribution of individual pay.

    The list median is the easy one to compute and the easy one to describe wrongly. Sorting the cells and taking the middle one gives you the middle cell. Writing “the typical job pays” over it claims the middle job, which this table cannot identify. Employment weighting still uses each group’s average. It does not reveal what the middle worker earns within or across those groups.

    The heaviest cell sitting below the cell median is Summit County plastics and rubber products at 1.01× on 3,824 jobs, the same cell the first recipe worked. One cell in 51 by the list count, and 11 percent of the jobs by the other. That is the gap between the two statistics, in one row.

    This site published the wrong sentence over the right number. The pay page read “the typical polymer job pays 1.2 times what the average job in its county pays” while the figure under it was a median over cells. The number was correct and the sentence was not, and a reader reported believing a stronger claim than the page was making. The fix was not a new number: it was naming the unit in the sentence. The weighted figure beside it also summarizes group averages; it is not a repair that recovers individual pay.

  4. What it lands on here

    In 2025, across twelve counties: the middle cell pays 1.21× what the average job in its county pays; the employment-weighted median of the broader group-average ratios is 1.26×. Against the same industries nationally, the median cell-average ratio is 0.88×. These are descriptive comparisons of group means, not evidence of an individual wage premium or a causal effect of choosing this industry.

    Publish the unit and the comparator in the sentence. Employment weighting gives larger groups more influence, but the underlying observations remain averages. The pay article also compares local manufacturing wages, a more relevant benchmark for a manufacturing job than every job in the county.

    Correction · 8 September 2026. This recipe called 1.26× the pay of the middle of 33,528 jobs. It is the employment-weighted median of county-industry average wage ratios. Individual median pay cannot be calculated from these group means.

The third worked question

Where do the people who work here actually live?

The labour shed is the area the people who work in a place actually live in. Most economic development offices have never pulled this one, which is the reason it is here: it answers the question a single county cannot, and it is the cleanest demonstration on this page that a place can have two totals that are both right. Four steps. It is a bulk download rather than an API, so read step one before you start.

  1. Take the origin-destination file, and know what it costs

    The Census Bureau’s LEHD programme publishes, for every state, a table of how many jobs are held by residents of each home area and worked in each work area. It is free, needs no key, and is a compressed bulk file rather than a query: one per state, tens of megabytes, and you will want a spreadsheet that copes or a few lines of code.

    https://lehd.ces.census.gov/data/lodes/LODES8/{st}/od/{st}_od_main_JT00_{year}.csv.gz

    main holds jobs where home and work are both in that state. aux is the other file, and it is the one people forget: jobs worked in the state by residents of somewhere else. Which of the two you take, or whether you take both, is the whole of step three.

    The industry cut in this file is three columns wide, and it is coarser than this site’s subject. Every row carries SI01, SI02 and SI03 beside the S000 total, and the three sum to it: goods-producing (NAICS 11, 21, 23 and 31-33), trade, transportation and utilities, and all other services. JT00 is the job-type flag, meaning all jobs rather than primary or private-only ones, and it is not a sector filter. No NAICS code of any width appears in the file, so no polymer labour shed can be built from it. A goods-producing one can, and SI01 is the column to take, but goods-producing is not manufacturing, and agriculture, mining and construction sit in that same column. Until 2026-09-01 this step said the file had no industry dimension at all and told you to stop looking for one.

  2. Know what one row is before you sum it

    one (home county, work county) count of JOBS, not people and not commuters. A two-job worker appears twice.

    A job is not a person and a home is not a journey. Somebody holding two jobs is two rows. The home end is an address on file, not evidence that anyone travelled: a fully remote worker who lives three states away and a worker who drives in every morning are the same row. Nothing in this file counts a trip, so do not call the output commuting.

  3. The choice that makes two right answers

    Now sum the jobs worked inside your region. Take only the main file and you have counted jobs held by residents of your own state. Add aux and you have counted jobs held by residents of anywhere. Both are defensible; they answer different questions, and they differ by more than a rounding.

    In 2022, this site’s twelve counties held 1,735,169 jobs counting a worker resident in any state, and 1,702,542 counting Ohio residents only. The two are 32,627 apart. Both were published here as the same footprint before anyone noticed they were two different measures.

    1,524,279 held from inside  +  178,263 from elsewhere in Ohio  =  1,702,542
    1,524,279 held from inside  +  210,890 from anywhere  =  1,735,169

    Notice what those two sums say: the jobs held from inside the region are the same number on both bases. Every bit of the difference sits in the imported half. So the two totals are not rival estimates of one quantity, and treating the gap as error is the mistake. Pick the basis your comparison needs, and if you are ranking yourself against other regions, use the basis they used.

  4. Read the outside half, and do not over-read it

    210,890 jobs are held from outside the twelve counties, which is 12.2 percent of everything worked here. Of those, 208,338 fall into three groups: 57,302 from counties next door, 61,708 from metropolitan areas hours away, and 89,328 from counties scattered too thinly to group, which is the largest of the three at 42.9 percent and the least examined. The other 2,552 are not in the grouping at all. That is 1.2 percent, small enough to round away and worth not rounding away: three numbers printed under a fourth read as a partition of it, and these are not one.

    Adjacent against distant is your judgment, not a published field. The file gives you a home county code and nothing else. Any grouping into neighbouring and far-away is a line you drew, so draw it explicitly, print the rule, and expect somebody to argue with it. The alternative is not objectivity; it is an unstated rule.

The decision under every comparison across years

A dollar in 2019 and a dollar in 2026 are different units

Both questions above are single-year, so nothing needed a basis. The moment you compare two years, you are choosing one whether you say so or not, and the default choice is the wrong one. This is the shortest thing on the page and it changes more answers than anything else on it.

  1. Pick a basis and name it in the sentence

    Nominal is the number as invoiced or appropriated: what actually changed hands. Real divides that by a price index so the unit stays the same size across years. Neither is more honest. What is dishonest is not saying which, because a reader assumes real and the easy number is nominal.

    This site shipped a whole page without making that call. Every series on its price page was nominal and the page never said so, across years in which general prices rose a quarter, so a rise of forty percent and a record high both read as real gains. Nothing was miscomputed. The basis was simply never stated, which is the most common way this goes wrong.

  2. Get the index, and use annual averages

    For general prices the standard choice is the Consumer Price Index for all urban consumers, annual averages, published free and without a key. Divide every figure by the index for its own year, then multiply by the index for the year you want to express everything in. Pick that base year once and print it.

    CPI-U, all items, U.S. city average, annual averages, 2019 to 2025, on a base of 2019. The index moved from 255.657 to 321.943, so general prices rose 25.9 percent across the window and a 2025 dollar buys what 79 cents bought in 2019. The divisor for any year is that year’s index over 2019’s: 2019 is 1, 2022 is 1.1447, 2025 is 1.2593.

    The 2025 average is eleven months, and the bureau says so. This series has no October 2025 value. It returns a dash for that month, with the footnote “Data unavailable due to the 2025 lapse in appropriations”, and the published annual average of 321.943 is the mean of the eleven months that do exist, to the digit. Use it, because it is what BLS published, but a completed year with a hole in it is not the same object as the other six, and a page that tells you to stamp your vintage should tell you that too. This step printed 322.132 until 2026-09-01.

    The index runs out before your data does. Annual averages exist only for completed years, so a figure from the current year has no deflator of its own. This example carries the 2025 index into 2026 as an approximation. Its direction of error depends on subsequent prices; it is not a guaranteed upper bound.

    Correction · 8 September 2026. The worked table and claim still used 322.132 after the prose had changed to 321.943. They now use the verified BLS observations shipped with the federal article: eleven available months in 2025. The corresponding rise is 25.9 percent; the worked finished-product real index below moves from 110.9 to 111.0.

  3. Now look at what moved

    Finished plastics and rubber prices. Up to 139.8 in cash against a 2019 base of 100, which is the rise of about forty percent that page reported. Deflated, 111.0: a real rise of about 11 percent, not forty. And the dearest month is not the same month. In cash it is July 2026; in real terms it was August 2022, nearly four years earlier. Resin makes the sharper case: 125.4 in cash still reads as a lasting increase, and at 99.6 deflated it has given back the entire rise and sits essentially back at its 2019 level.

    The peak is the one that catches people. A series at a record high in cash can have peaked years earlier in real terms, and a page that says “record high” without a basis has told a reader something that is true of the money and false of the value. That is not a rounding difference. It is a different sentence.

  4. Which of your own statements survive

    Take every sentence you have written that compares two years and re-run it on the deflated series. Three things can happen: it survives unchanged, it survives with a smaller number, or it reverses. Print which. A finding that holds on both bases is worth more than one that holds on the one you happened to pick, and you only know which you have if you check.

    That page’s central finding is an ORDERING, that the cost spike was given back at the wellhead, partly at resin, and not at all in finished products. Re-run on the deflated series the ordering holds, because every link faces the same general inflation. So the page may stay nominal and says that it rechecked. Its magnitudes did not survive: forty percent became 11, and a record high became a four-year-old peak. Both outcomes in one page, which is why the check is worth running rather than reasoning about.

The fourth worked question

How much federal money reaches your industry, in your place?

This one teaches the limit of a code filter better than anything else on the page, because the thing it gets wrong is invisible in its own output. Four steps, and the fourth is the one that changes what you publish.

  1. Ask by category, with a POST

    USAspending publishes every federal award. The endpoint that answers “how much, cut by X” takes a POST with a JSON body, and the category is part of the path rather than the body: naics and county are the two this site asks for.

    POST https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/search/spending_by_category/naics/

    Filter on place of performance for your counties and on the fiscal years you want. Select prime-contract award types A–D for the manufacturing view. Follow every response’s pagination metadata until it reports no next page; filtering the first page alone can discard smaller categories. Retain negative obligations, which record de-obligations. No key, no registration.

  2. Decide which years you are allowed to compare

    This site pulls fiscal 2019 to 2026, eight years, of which seven are finished. Across the finished years the chemical and plastics/rubber prime-contract view totals $289.5M in 2025 dollars. Adding the part-year brings it to $313.6M; $24.2M is however much of FY2026 had elapsed when the file was read on 8 September 2026. For reconciliation with the source, the nominal totals are $254.4M closed and $278.6M including the partial year.

    The current fiscal year is not a year. It is however much of a year has elapsed, and putting it in a bar chart beside completed years draws a collapse that has not happened. Either exclude it or label it, and say which you did.

    And these are dollars across years, so the basis section above applies. Federal award data is published nominal. A rising total across seven years is partly prices.

  3. An obligation is not an outlay, and a place is not an observation

    Place of performance is a reported field on the award, not an observation of where the work happened. A centrally administered contract, or a prime performing through a subaward, can put the code somewhere other than the activity. An obligation is not an outlay.

    An obligation is money a signed award commits the government to pay. An outlay is money that has actually left the Treasury. The totals this recipe returns are obligations, so they say what was committed and never what has been paid.

  4. Now name what your filter cannot see

    Research grants are excluded by the prime-contract award-type filter, A–D. Contract NAICS classifies the supplies or services acquired; institutional identity does not determine that code. This view also selects 325*/326* manufacturing codes. The NSF NEO-SMART Engine ($14,999,983) and TARDISS do not appear in the NAICS rows. The all-type county view can include grants whose reported performance location is inside the footprint. This cut sees 37 industry codes, led by all other rubber product manufacturing at $87.6M, tire manufacturing (except retreading) at $80.1M, and paint and coating manufacturing at $23.6M in nominal source dollars across the full window, including partial FY2026. Everything else in the region’s federal money is outside the frame by construction, and the total does not know that.

    This is the step people skip, and it is the reason to run this recipe at all. An industry filter returns a number, and the number is correct for what it counted. Nothing in the output says a whole class of award was never eligible. Write the sentence naming what your filter excludes and print it next to the total, every time, because a reader cannot infer it and you will forget it within a month.

Complete pagination yields 37 codes, $254.4M closed and $278.6M including the partial year. The comparison above now uses constant 2025 dollars; nominal amounts remain labelled for audit. The exclusion explanation also used institutional industry codes: grants are excluded here by award type, while contract NAICS identifies what is purchased.

Checking a single number, including ours

Any dollar figure with an award behind it can be checked in about a minute

Everything above this is a statistic built from a census. This is the opposite: one award, one identifier, one record, and no judgement calls at all. It is here because it is the only part of this page that teaches you to check us, and because the worked example is the largest error this site has published.

  1. Get the identifier, and expect a page not to print it

    Every federal award carries an identifier that is stable, public and unique. Most announcements quote a dollar figure and omit it, which is the point at which a number stops being checkable and becomes a claim. If a page you are reading gives a federal figure and no identifier, that is a finding about the page.

    The EDA Sustainable Polymers Tech Hub is seven implementation awards from the U.S. Economic Development Administration, $51,001,413 between them. Each carries its own identifier, and the seven amounts add to the total, so a reader can check the addition as well as the parts.

    The fiscal year in an identifier is the year of the award action, not the year of the programme. Two of the seven above carry ED24 rather than ED25, on the same designation. Sorting or filtering by the digits in an id will silently split a programme in half.

  2. Look it up at the agency, not at a press release

    NSF is the easy one. Its records are keyed by the same number that appears in the announcement, need no key and no registration, and open in a browser.

    https://api.nsf.gov/services/v1/awards.json?id={award_id}

    The figures you came for are named estimatedTotalAmt and fundsObligatedAmt, with fundsObligated breaking the obligation down by fiscal year, and transType telling you whether it is a grant or a cooperative agreement.

    USAspending takes two steps, and the obvious one fails silently. The award number printed on a federal grant document is not the key its award endpoint takes: that endpoint wants USAspending’s own internal identifier, and a real award number handed to it directly returns a bare 404 rather than an error that explains itself. Ask the search endpoint for the internal id first. It is a POST, and it is a different endpoint from the {category} paths in the register below.

    POST https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/search/spending_by_award/
{"filters": {"award_ids": ["ED25HDQ0G0038"],
             "award_type_codes": ["02", "03", "04", "05"]},
 "fields": ["Award ID", "Recipient Name", "Award Amount", "generated_internal_id"]}

    That reply carries generated_internal_id, and it carries Award Amount as well, which is often the whole answer: for the first award in the table above it comes back as 11,122,386, the figure printed beside it. Feed the internal id to the award endpoint for the full record, where the obligation is total_obligation and the number you started from is fain.

    https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/awards/{generated_internal_id}/

    Neither agency needs registration. This is the cheapest verification on the whole page and the one least often done.

  3. Three numbers that are not each other

    An award record carries several dollar figures and an announcement will quote whichever is largest. They mean different things and only one of them is money that has actually moved.

    NSF award 2532460, made 13 July 2026, runs 2026 to 2036. Its record gives an estimated total of $14,999,983 and $7,499,984 obligated FY2026, which is 50 percent of it. The awardee describes this same award as reaching up to $160 million over ten years, phased and contingent on milestones: $7.5 million in each of the first two years, $15 million in each of the next three and $20 million in each of the last five. That schedule sums to $160 million exactly, and its first tranche is the $7,499,984 the record shows obligated. The ceiling is real, it belongs to this award, and none of it beyond the first tranche is committed.

    Obligated is money legally committed, and it is the only one with a number behind it today. Estimated total is what the award is expected to reach across the period the record covers if every year is funded. A contingent ceiling is the largest the award could ever reach if every milestone is met and every year is appropriated. It is a forecast with conditions attached, not a sum anybody holds, and it appears in press material because it is the biggest available number. All three can be true of one award at once, which is exactly why a figure copied from an announcement needs the record beside it.

  4. The one this site got wrong

    This site printed $160M for that award. The record says $14,999,983 estimated and $7,499,984 obligated. The $160M is not invented and it is not someone else’s: it is this award’s own ten-year contingent ceiling. What we did was print a conditional maximum as though it were a value, with no date range and no condition attached, on a site whose premise is that its numbers can be checked. Set against the estimated total in the record it is 10.7 times larger; set against what is actually committed today it is 21 times larger. It stood until 29 August 2026.

    It was in a hand-typed event title, taken from a description rather than a record, and it sat on this site until a reader put two of our own pages side by side and found the same award carrying two figures an order of magnitude apart. No gate caught it, because no gate was comparing figures across pages; one does now. The lookup above would have caught it in a minute, at any point, by anybody.

    This is the section to copy. Not because the mistake is unusual, but because it is ordinary: a ceiling read as a value, from a description rather than a record, on the one number nobody thought to check because it was ours.

The part that is worth copying without our code

Each registered claim names the condition under which it stops matching its source

One idea, and you do not need our tooling to use it. It is here because it is the cheapest thing on this page to adopt and the only one that keeps working after the person who built the analysis has moved on.

Across the twenty-two article pages, excluding the hub, this tally carries 526 claims. The hub adds 29, making 555 across all 23 site pages. Of the 526 article claims, 504 are re-run against the shipped data in the verification suite, so a registered figure that stops matching its assertion fails that check. The other 22 rest on a person having read a document correctly, which is why they are labelled and are the place to attack first. This page alone carries 34.

What the conditions prove, and what they do not. They run against the data a page shipped with, so a green board means the registered assertions pass against those files. It does not mean the file is right about the world, and it does not mean the sentence reads the file correctly.

The discipline, not the code. Write the sentence you intend to publish. Then, beside it, write the condition under which it would be false, as an expression over the data rather than as a note to yourself: not “check this later” but “this is wrong if the median moves outside this band” or “this is wrong if these three counts stop summing to that one”. Re-run them whenever the data changes. A spreadsheet column of TRUE and FALSE does the job; the mechanism does not matter and the habit does.

On this page, in one week: a recipe that printed three aggregation codes returning nothing from the file it told you to download; a median described over the wrong unit; three published figures asserted to sum to a fourth that they miss by 2,552; and a source credited on a page that never used it, which had quietly made one dataset look more central than it is. The first three were caught by a condition written next to the sentence. The last was caught by a person reading the page.

The one that got through. On an unpublished page in this series, one transposed federal classification code erased a university’s degrees from a finding, and the condition beside the sentence kept passing: it tested that the last value sat below three quarters of the peak, and that was true of the wrong number and of the right one. The sentence was out by half. Figures here are recomputed from the source data by script rather than typed, and every reading of what they mean is sent to competing AI systems to be argued down before publication. None of that found it.

It does not make a page right. A check is only as good as the question it asks, and the failures on this site that hurt most were the ones no check was looking for: a filter that was valid for a different geography, a source credited on a page that never used it, three published numbers that did not sum to a fourth. Every one was found by a person reading the page. Write the conditions anyway, and do not mistake a green board for a correct one.

When it does not reproduce

You followed it and got a different number

Assume the difference is real and find it, rather than assuming one of us made an arithmetic mistake. Nearly every failure to reproduce a figure on this site has turned out to be one of five things, in roughly this order of frequency. Work down the list; the first four are cheap to check.

  1. A different vintage

    The series was revised between our pull and yours. Federal series restate history routinely and quietly. Every figure here is stamped with the date it was read; compare that to your own pull date before anything else. A small difference in a recent month is almost always this.

  2. A different basis

    The commonest cause of a difference too large to be revision. Same data, different denominator or different population: private against all ownerships, one state’s residents against any state’s, nominal against real, a median over rows against a median over jobs. Each of those is worked through above, and each produces a number that is not wrong.

  3. A different code set

    You included a code we excluded, or summed a family together with its own parts. The second one is silent: nothing in the output looks wrong. Check your hierarchy before your arithmetic.

  4. A suppression you read as a zero

    A blank cell is withheld, not zero. If your total is lower than ours, count how many cells you have against how many we say we had.

  5. A different geography

    County, metropolitan area and state are published separately. Metros overlapping a county region do not necessarily sum to it, and a custom region is yours to build rather than the bureau’s to publish.

If it is none of these, we would rather know. A figure here that cannot be reproduced from public data is a defect whether or not the arithmetic is right, because the whole claim of this page is that a stranger can follow it. The address to argue with is at the foot of the page, and the most useful note names the sentence and what you think it fails against.

The decision nobody publishes

Federal data makes you define your subject inside someone else’s classification, once per question

There is no polymer industry, no polymer occupation and no polymer degree in federal statistics. There are code lists, and somebody has to decide which codes are the subject. This site makes that decision three times, in three taxonomies, and every one of them is a judgement with a consequence rather than a lookup.

Add an industry family to its own parts and the same job is in the table twice

Jobs in the twelve counties this site measures, 2025, as the pay page publishes them. Each part code sits inside the family code above it, so a reader who adds the whole column gets a number that does not exist. The top bar is that number; the bottom bar is the same jobs counted once. Bars start at zero.

The boundary call, with its price on it

The education codes are the clearest case, because this site already wrote its boundary down. Programmes in polymer and plastics are core. Materials, mechanical, chemical and general chemistry programmes are adjacent: they feed the same employers and they are not the same thing. The site keeps the two lists separately rather than merging them, which means the judgement is inspectable instead of baked in.

Counting only the three core codes, the region’s universities awarded 1,175 degrees between 2014 and 2023, across eight programmes. Count the adjacent codes too and it is 1,432 across thirteen: a pipeline 22% larger, from the same institutions in the same years, because a line moved.

Neither answer is wrong. Publishing one without saying which line was drawn is.

Geography is the fourth classification, and it is the one that breaks people

County, metropolitan area and state are three separate geographies, published separately, and they do not sum into one another. A metro is a set of whole counties. Under the July 2023 delineation, four metros overlap PIC-12: Akron, Canton-Massillon, Cleveland and Youngstown-Warren. Akron, Cleveland and Youngstown-Warren lie entirely inside PIC-12. Canton-Massillon also includes Carroll County, outside PIC-12; Wayne County belongs to the Wooster micropolitan area. These areas therefore do not tile PIC-12 exactly. That forbids three things outright: adding metro figures to get a region, treating a metro price level as a county price level, and putting a county figure in the same sentence as a metro figure without naming both. This site has already had a defect from crossing that line, which is why the geography a page is built on is stamped on the page in a banner rather than left in a footnote.

How to do this for your own industry

  1. Start from the plants, not the codes

    List the employers everyone in the region agrees are the industry, then look up what each one actually files under. The code list falls out of the firms; a code list drawn first will miss them.

    Where to look each one up. Three routes, none of which needs a key. Census publishes the classification itself, with definitions and the full hierarchy, at census.gov/naics, and its search box takes a plain description of what a plant makes. For a named firm, the federal award record carries the code the government itself assigned: search the company at usaspending.gov and read naics_code off any award. For a public company, its SIC is on the cover of every SEC filing at sec.gov/edgar, which is a different scheme but close enough to tell you which family you are in.

    Then test each candidate code with one question: if a plant filed under this code closed tomorrow, would anyone in the region call it a loss to this industry? Yes means keep it, no means it is context, and cannot-tell means it is a boundary call and belongs in the next step rather than in the main list. That is the test that put 325 outside this site’s own register and 3252 inside it. The concentration page still draws 325, but beside the cluster as context rather than as part of it.

  2. Write down the boundary calls as calls

    For every code somebody reasonable would argue about, keep it in a separate named group, the way the education codes here are split into core and adjacent. Then either answer can be produced on demand.

  3. Draw the hierarchy before you sum anything

    Note which of your codes contain which. A two-minute drawing prevents the double count.

  4. Say what the choice makes invisible

    Every code filter is a decision about what you will not be able to see. Industry-coded federal award data cannot see a university or a research institute, because they file under entirely different codes, so a whole class of award is missing by construction rather than by accident. Publish that sentence next to the number.

  5. Publish the list

    The codes cost you weeks and cost a reader nothing. A methodology that names the dataset without naming the codes tells somebody where to start, not what you did.

The trap

A withheld cell is not a zero

Every statistical agency suppresses cells that would identify an employer, and the smaller the geography, the more of them go. Nothing in the file says withheld in a way a spreadsheet respects: the cell is blank, or it is a zero, or it carries a one-letter disclosure code most people never read. Someone who treats those as real zeroes gets an answer that looks fine and is wrong, which is worse than an answer that looks wrong.

Every state discloses. Most metropolitan areas and most counties do not

Each bar is every area of that kind in the country, drawn the same length and split into the areas with a published employment figure for plastics and rubber products and the areas where it was withheld. The count of areas is in the row label, because equal-length bars deliberately say nothing about it.

Count these yourself. The same census slices the other way, one file per industry across every area in the country, and that file is where these rates come from:

https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/{year}/a/industry/{naics}.csv

Take own_code 5, which is private ownership, and count the rows whose disclosure_code is N, the letter the bureau prints where it has withheld a cell. County rows are agglvl_code 75, metropolitan rows 45 and state rows 55. The figures drawn here are the 2024 annual file, and the year is load-bearing: the 2025 file gives 724 disclosed counties of 1,839 and carries no metropolitan rows at all yet, read 2026-08-29. A reader who pulls the newest year and compares it to this chart is looking at a different vintage, not at a disagreement.

What to do about it. Three things, in order. Read the disclosure code rather than the value, and carry it through your pipeline. Say “among disclosed areas” every time you state a rank, because a rank computed over the visible half is not a national rank. And never let a filter that returns nothing render as a finding of zero: a code that comes back empty in every year at every geography is far more likely to be a bad code than a real absence. This site published a transposed programme code once and erased a whole university’s degree programme, and every check passed, because the checks were true of the data the mistake had already filtered out.

Before you tell us we are wrong

Your number will not match ours, and that is expected

Federal series revise for years after first publication. A figure pulled today is a figure as of today, and the version this site printed was as of the day it was pulled, which is why every page here stamps a date in its masthead. If you re-run one of these recipes and land a little away from us, the first thing to check is not the arithmetic.

This site measured it, on three producer-price series it publishes: 259 of the 273 published months were changed after they were first printed, by a typical 0.15%. The largest single revision on record there was 1.41%. Small, and not nothing: it is enough to move a figure quoted to one decimal place, and it is why a number here is a number as of a date rather than a number.

So: stamp your own pull date on your own output, keep the raw file you pulled, and when you compare against somebody else’s figure, compare the vintages before you compare the numbers. A difference of a fraction of a percent against a published figure is the series behaving normally. A difference of a factor is a code or a geography or an ownership filter, and it is almost always the code.
