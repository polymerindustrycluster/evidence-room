Where Northeast Ohio actually sits

Ohio is first in the country in plastics and rubber employment.

Ohio’s first place rests on 54,846 plastics and rubber jobs at private employers in 2024. All 51 state figures are published, the 50 states and the District of Columbia, so the state ranking is complete. Metro claims need more care: the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) withholds the job figure for 227 of the country’s 382 metro areas.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data: the federal Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW), annual averages, 2015–2024 · August 2026

The clean claim

The top 20 states, and Ohio is first with no asterisk

States are the one geography BLS publishes in full: 51 of 51 disclosed, none withheld.

QCEW is the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, the federal count of jobs covered by unemployment insurance. NAICS 326 is the industry code for plastics and rubber products manufacturing, which is what every number on this page counts. Withheld (suppressed is the bureau’s own word for it): BLS holds a figure back when so few employers sit behind it that printing it would identify one of them. A cell is one such figure, for one area in one year in one industry. The jobs in a withheld cell are real; the number is not disclosed, which is not the same as zero. An establishment is one physical site, not one company. A metro area is the federal statistical region built out of whole counties around a city. The Akron metro is two counties, Summit and Portage, and their job figures come to its metro total of 6,910. They reconcile because the metro is exactly those two counties and nothing else. Counties and metro areas are still separate published geographies, each with its own totals, so this page never adds a county figure to a metro one. Concentration asks how much of the local job base an industry fills next to how much it fills nationally. Above 1.0× is more than the country, below 1.0× is less. Akron’s 4.69× is nearly five times the national slice. That is what the cluster is built on. It is also how much of the local job base rides on one industry: only seven of the 155 metros BLS discloses are more concentrated than Akron. Private ownership counts private employers only, leaving out federal, state and local government jobs. Annual averages are the twelve monthly counts averaged.

The narrow claim

Akron and Cleveland among the metros we can see

Measure the same 155 metros BLS discloses two ways. Seventeen of them carry 4,000 or more plastics and rubber jobs. In 37 of them, the industry fills at least twice as much of the local job base as it does of the country’s. Seven metros are large and concentrated at once, and Akron is one. Among the six disclosed metros with the most plastics and rubber jobs, Akron is also the most concentrated. Cleveland is the other half of the cluster’s story, bigger on jobs and much less concentrated, which is what a large diversified metro looks like in this industry.

Who else is up there

The company Akron keeps is six factory towns

Six other metros are large and concentrated at once, and none of them is a big city. Elkhart, Indiana builds most of the country’s recreational vehicles. Hickory, North Carolina makes furniture and fiber-optic cable. Erie, Pennsylvania runs injection molding and mold-making shops. Greenville, South Carolina holds Michelin’s North American headquarters and the only US assembly plant of BMW (the German carmaker). Scranton and Greensboro fill out the seven. What those towns make is not in the BLS file: it comes from the companies’ and trade groups’ own public statements, and it is the one passage here we cannot re-check against the data. Akron earns its place in that group the way the other six do, on its job count and its concentration. Its 6,910 jobs sit across 117 establishments, which is a plant of about 59 people, repeated 117 times.
