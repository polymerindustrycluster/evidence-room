Polymer Industry Cluster Cluster vitals · concentration

Which industry, and by how much?

Paint is six times as big a share of the jobs here as it is of the country’s.

That is 5.96× the national share, the strongest of six polymer industries and more than double the 2.44× of the rubber products the region is named for. Paint has led all six in every year since 2015. A reading that high means more coatings work per job here than the country has, in sites, in suppliers and in people who know it; it does not mean the industry is big. Paint is 4,259 jobs in a region of 1.70 million, about one in four hundred. Every figure is rebuilt from free federal files and matches the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ own published figure to the last digit it prints.

The finding

Paint leads all six polymer industries, and has every year since 2015

Every figure on this page compares two shares: how much of the region’s jobs sit in an industry, against how much of the country’s do. At 1.0× the two match. Above 1.0× the industry is more concentrated here than nationally, and below it, less. Economists call this a location quotient. Paint sits far above that line: above 5.4× for eleven straight years, and more than double whichever industry is second. Ohio as a whole is already concentrated in paint at 3.78×, and these twelve counties are well above the state.

How big is that in jobs? The whole cluster this page measures, resin plus paint plus plastics and rubber products, comes to 24,030 of the region’s 1.70 million jobs, about one in seventy, and it is a floor: the bureau will not publish every county, so the true figure is higher. Paint is 4,259 of those, 18%. The figures come from the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, or QCEW, which counts jobs covered by unemployment insurance and reports them as annual averages of the year’s four quarters; the analysis is John Swanson’s, and every figure in it is re-run against the bureau’s own published one in the check at the foot of this page.

Where the paint is

One county holds three of every four paint jobs the bureau publishes

Each cell is one industry in one county, latest year. A cell at 1.0× holds exactly its national share of those jobs; at 11.2×, eleven times as much. Counties are sorted by the paint column, highest first, so the county this section is about is the top row; the counties whose paint cell is withheld sit beneath, ordered by plastics and rubber. Hatched cells are withheld and are never drawn as a zero.

Who is in that cell. The file counts separate sites, not companies: one physical location is one site, so a firm with a head office and two plants in the region counts three times. It never names an employer, so this grid shows a concentration and not a firm. Sherwin-Williams is headquartered in Cleveland, in Cuyahoga County, and RPM International in Medina, which posts the region’s fifth paint reading at 2.9×. A site is coded by what it does, so a corporate campus can land in the same industry as a plant: the plain explanation for a coatings cluster of this size, and not something the file measures.

The honest caveat

The highest concentrations sit on the fewest jobs

Each dot is one industry in one county, latest year. The further UP, the more concentrated that industry is here than in the country. The further RIGHT, the more jobs sit behind that number. The job axis is squeezed so the small counts stay apart, and the ticks are spaced to match: twice as far right is four times as many jobs, not twice as many.

Checked against the bureau

Every cell lands inside the bureau’s own rounding

Each bar counts how many of our figures landed that far from the bureau’s published one. Zero is an exact match, and every bar sits inside the bureau’s own rounding. Read the shape this way: an error in the method would pile the bars up on one side of zero, and a difference that is only rounding spreads them evenly across the range, which is what happened.
