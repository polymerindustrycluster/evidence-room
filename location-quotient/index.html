Which industry, and by how much?

Paint is six times as big a share of the jobs here as it is of the country’s.

Paint’s share of the jobs in the twelve Northeast Ohio counties this page measures is 5.96× its share of the country’s, the strongest of the three industries counted here as the cluster: resin, paint, and plastics and rubber products. It is more than double the 2.44× of rubber products, the work Akron is named for, which is part of that third industry rather than a fourth one, and paint has led every year since 2015. A share that high means the industry is dense here rather than large: more paint sites, more suppliers and more people who know the work than a region this size would usually hold. Paint is 4,259 jobs in a region of 1.70 million, about one in four hundred. Every figure is rebuilt from free federal files, and where the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) publishes its own, the two agree to every digit the bureau prints.

The finding

Paint leads the three cluster industries, and has every year since 2015

Every reading with a × on this page compares two shares: how much of the region’s jobs sit in an industry, against how much of the country’s do. At 1.0× the two match. Above 1.0× the industry is more concentrated here than nationally, and below it, less. Economists call this a location quotient. Paint sits far above that mark: above 5.4× for eleven straight years, and more than double whichever line is next. Ohio as a whole is already concentrated in paint at 3.78×, and these twelve counties are well above the state.

How big is that in jobs? The whole cluster this page measures, resin plus paint plus plastics and rubber products, comes to 24,030 of the region’s 1.70 million jobs, about one in seventy. Paint is 4,259 of those, 18%. Both counts are floors: the bureau will not publish every county, so the true figures are higher. The figures come from the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, or QCEW, which counts jobs covered by unemployment insurance and reports them as annual averages of the year’s four quarters; the analysis is John Swanson’s, and every figure in it is re-run against the bureau’s own published one in the check at the foot of this page.

Each line is one industry, summed across the counties the bureau discloses. The higher the line, the more concentrated that industry is here than in the country as a whole. Paint is the dark line, drawn forward, and the other five are grey. Three of the six are the cluster: resin, paint, and plastics and rubber products. Two more, plastics products and rubber products, are slices of that third line rather than industries standing beside it. The sixth, chemical manufacturing, sits outside the cluster and is drawn only for comparison. So the six lines are not one ranking. Resin climbed hardest of the five greys, from 1.33× in 2015 to 2.00× now. Those two readings sit on different county sets, six counties then and five now, with as few as four in between, so part of that climb is the bureau publishing more of the industry rather than the region holding more of it. Hold the set still and the climb is real but smaller in proportion: on the four counties disclosed in every year, resin goes from 5.04× in 2015 to 6.45× now, on that narrower base. Rubber products and plastics products both end below where they started.

Where the paint is

One county holds three of every four paint jobs the bureau publishes

Each cell is one industry in one county, latest year. A cell at 1.0× holds exactly its national share of those jobs; at 11.2×, eleven times as much. Counties are sorted by the paint column, highest first, so Cuyahoga, the county this section is about, is the top row; the counties whose paint cell is withheld sit beneath, ordered by plastics and rubber. Hatched cells are withheld and are never drawn as a zero.

Who is in that cell. The federal jobs file counts separate sites, not companies: one physical location is one site, so a firm with a head office and two plants in the region counts three times. It never names an employer, so this grid shows a concentration and not a firm. Sherwin-Williams is headquartered in Cleveland, in Cuyahoga County, and RPM International in Medina, which posts the region’s fifth paint reading at 2.9×. A site is coded by what it does, so a corporate campus can land in the same industry as a plant: the plain explanation for a coatings cluster of this size, and not something the file measures.

The honest caveat

The highest concentrations sit on the fewest jobs

Each dot is one industry in one county, latest year. The further UP, the more concentrated that industry is here than in the country. The further RIGHT, the more jobs sit behind that number. The job axis is squeezed so the small counts stay apart, and the ticks are spaced to match: twice as far right is four times as many jobs, not twice as many.

Checked against the bureau

Every cell lands inside the bureau’s own rounding

Each bar counts how many of our figures landed that far from the bureau’s published one. Zero is an exact match, and every bar sits inside the bureau’s own rounding. Read the shape this way: an error in the method would pile the bars up on one side of zero, and a difference that is only rounding spreads them evenly across the range, which is what happened.
