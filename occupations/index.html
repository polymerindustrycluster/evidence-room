Staffing: the whole country · Pay and degrees: Northeast Ohio

Polymer manufacturing runs on production jobs.

Half the nation’s molding-machine setters work in plastics and rubber manufacturing. Across four Northeast Ohio metros, degree occupations have the wider typical pay gap against national medians. Selected polymer programs at Akron and Case Western Reserve conferred 63 degrees in 2023, against an annual average of 142 in 2014–2020. These are staffing, pay and education measures with different scopes.

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data BLS, O*NET and IPEDS · August 2026

All four figures below count the whole country, not Northeast Ohio.

The staffing pattern United States

The fourteen largest occupations are 60% of the industry, and most of them are on the floor

Start with who does the work, across the whole country. Almost nobody outside this industry builds a tire: 95% of the country’s tire builders work inside it. The two occupations most nearly confined to this industry are both floor jobs.

What each pays Northeast Ohio against the nation

The pay gap against the nation is widest at the top of the schooling ladder

Here the page changes scope: from this section on, the dots are Northeast Ohio metros and the diamonds are the same job nationally. Those floor jobs are paid near the national rate in Akron and Cleveland. The shortfall concentrates where the region claims distinction: in every metro, the six occupations whose workers mostly hold degrees sit further under their national medians than the fourteen high-school-majority occupations. The median is the middle wage, half the jobs in that metro paying more and half less. Only 5 of Akron’s 25 published occupations are paid above the same job nationally, 11 of 25 in Cleveland, 5 of 22 in Canton-Massillon and 1 of 21 in Youngstown-Warren. Why the discount lands on exactly these jobs is a question these files cannot answer.

The degree band sits deeper under its diamonds than the floor band in every metro

Annual median wage, May 2024, across every industry that employs the occupation. One row is one job: the hollow diamond is what it pays nationally, and a metro’s dot to the LEFT of that diamond means the metro pays the job less than the country does. A thirty-dollar gap and a sixteen-thousand-dollar gap look alike at this scale, so each row also carries its metro median divided by the national one, printed to the right of the dots and headed with the metro it belongs to: 0.93 is 7% under the nation, 1.01 is 1% over. Rows are grouped by the schooling workers report (next section) and ordered by the national median inside each group, and each band’s summary is the middle of that band’s own ratios, never an average of dollars.

What the gap means in a paycheck Akron against the nation

Thirty dollars at the bottom of the ladder, sixteen thousand at the top

A molding-machine setter in metro Akron makes $41,260 a year at the median, thirty dollars over the same job’s national median. A chemical engineer in the same metro makes $105,660 against $121,860 nationally, about $16,200 a year under. That $16,200 is 13% of the national figure, wider than the 7% written on the pay chart’s degree band, because 7% is the middle of Akron’s six degree occupations and chemical engineers are the second-widest of the six. These are medians across every employer in each metro, not offers from any one plant, and they are the whole page in two paychecks.

What schooling the work reports United States

Six of these occupations are degree jobs; fourteen are high-school-majority; the rest sit between

Scope changes back: this survey has no Ohio cut, so these are national figures used to sort the rows on both charts. The degree band above is defined by what workers themselves report. Each bar splits one occupation’s surveyed workers and occupational experts by the education they say the job requires. The label at the right is the occupation’s Job Zone, the Labor Department’s rating of how much overall preparation the work needs, running from 1 for the least to 5 for the most. The six degree-majority occupations are the engineer and scientist jobs, exactly the rows the metros pay furthest under the nation.

Every degree-majority occupation is an engineer or scientist job

Share of respondents reporting each level as what the job requires, from O*NET 30.3, the Labor Department’s occupational database, which has no Ohio cut: these are national figures. Every bar runs the full width, so what varies is where the dark begins: the earlier it begins, the more schooling the job’s own people say it takes. Where the degree jobs are trained Northeast Ohio

Two universities in this file confer the polymer degrees, and the count has fallen below half its old pace

The degree jobs the metros underpay draw their local supply from two universities, and a third confers the adjacent materials degrees. A fourth university inside the twelve counties is not in these charts: Youngstown State, in Mahoning County, confers materials science doctorates under federal code 40.1001, the same code Kent State’s materials rows use. The completions query behind this section names three institutions rather than asking the region, so the materials comparison group is a floor rather than a count of the region, and the universities counted here are the universities in the file. What is missing is small: one to four doctorates in most years, against a materials group that runs from 20 to 34. Youngstown State has no figure at all for 2022, the year the federal mirror skipped and this build fills in for its three named institutions only. The federal completions record shows polymer programs conferring between 118 and 179 degrees a year from 2014 through 2020, an average of 142, then 54 in 2021, 62 in 2022 and 63 in 2023. Half that average is 71 a year, which is the rule drawn on the chart, and all three later years sit under it. The last two rose off the 2021 low and neither reached the rule. Three years is still a short record, and a program recoded to a different federal program code would leave these counts without leaving the region. The adjacent materials programs, meaning materials science and materials engineering at the same universities, held their usual range through the same three years.

Polymer degrees fell below half their 2014–2020 pace

Degrees conferred a year by the University of Akron, Case Western and Kent State, polymer programs against the adjacent materials programs carried as a comparison group. The rule is half the 142-a-year polymer average for 2014–2020, so a polymer column under it is a year these programs conferred less than half their earlier average.

The University of Akron confers 62% of the polymer degrees in this file

Degrees conferred a year, average of 2021–2023, by program and institution. Darker bars: polymer programs; lighter: adjacent materials programs. In this file, Akron and Case Western confer polymer degrees; Kent State appears here among the materials programs alone.

Production jobs dominate the national industry. Akron’s pay gap is wider among degree occupations, while selected polymer programs confer fewer degrees.

Akron’s median wage ratio is 3% below the national rate for high-school-majority occupations and 7% below it for degree occupations, or 0.97× and 0.93×. The polymer programs counted here conferred 63 degrees in 2023, against an average of 142 a year in 2014–2020. These files do not measure recruiting reach, graduates staying in the region or a worker shortage.
