The jobs inside plastics and rubber manufacturing — nationally, and what they pay in four Northeast Ohio metros

In plastics and rubber manufacturing, one job in nine is a molding-machine setter — and half of all the nation’s molding-machine setters work in this one industry.

PIC’s workforce case is usually argued in totals: how many jobs, what the average job pays. This page opens the total up. It names the occupations the industry is actually staffed with, what each pays in Akron, Cleveland, Canton-Massillon and Youngstown-Warren against the same job nationally, and what schooling the people doing the work report having. Two limits are stated here, not in a footnote. The staffing shares are national and the metro wages are for every industry that employs the occupation, not for plastics plants alone; and the metros neither nest inside nor tile the twelve counties PIC measures against, so they are shown side by side and never summed.

The staffing pattern

Fourteen occupations are 59% of the industry, and most of them are on the floor

Each bar is one occupation’s share of everyone employed in plastics and rubber manufacturing nationally in 2024. The figure at the bar’s end is the reverse question — what share of that occupation, across every industry, works in this one — which is how polymer-specific the job is. Bars encode from zero and the axis is linear.

What each pays

Against the same job nationally, the region’s metros mostly pay less — and the gap is widest at the top

One row per occupation, ordered by the national annual median. The hollow diamond is the nation; the dots are the three metros with the most published cells. A dot left of the diamond pays under the national median for that job; right of it, over. These are dollars as paid, in every industry that employs the occupation, and not adjusted for what a dollar buys in each metro.

What schooling the work reports

Six of these occupations are degree jobs; fourteen are high-school jobs; the rest sit between

Each bar is one occupation, and its four segments are the share of surveyed workers and occupational experts who report each level of education as what the job requires — high school or less, some college or an associate degree, a bachelor’s, a graduate degree. The label at the right is the occupation’s Job Zone, the Labor Department’s rating of how much overall preparation the work typically needs, from little to extensive. Neither is a hiring requirement.

Where the degree jobs are trained here

Three universities confer the region’s polymer and materials degrees

For the six occupations above where a majority hold a bachelor’s or more, the local supply is the degrees conferred by the region’s universities in polymer engineering, polymer chemistry, materials engineering and materials science. Each bar is one program at one institution — the average number of degrees conferred a year across the latest three years the federal record covers. Programs, not people.

The industry’s jobs are mostly floor jobs, and Akron and Cleveland pay them within a few percent of the national rate. The degree jobs are few, pay under the national rate in every metro, and are trained at three schools.
