Staffing: the whole country · Pay and degrees: Northeast Ohio

The region staffs from the floor, pays the top under the nation, and now trains half as many.

Half the nation’s molding-machine setters work in this one industry, and floor work is the shape of it across the whole United States. Only the pay and the degrees on this page are Northeast Ohio, where all four metros pay the degree occupations under the national rate and the polymer programs conferred 63 degrees in 2023 against an average of 139 a year through 2021. Can the region staff, pay and replace the jobs that make it a polymer capital?

By John Swanson, Polymer Industry Cluster desk · Analysis and graphics by Claude (Anthropic) · Data BLS, O*NET and IPEDS · August 2026

All four figures below count the whole country, not Northeast Ohio.

The staffing pattern United States

The fourteen largest occupations are 60% of the industry, and most of them are on the floor

Start with who does the work, across the whole country. Tire builders read 95%, which is to say almost nobody in the United States builds a tire outside this industry, and the two jobs most nearly confined to it are both floor jobs.

The largest floor job alone outweighs every engineer and scientist in the industry combined

Every number on this chart counts the United States. Bar length is the occupation’s share of everyone employed in plastics and rubber manufacturing in 2024. The figure at the right asks the reverse question: of everyone in the country who holds that job, in any industry, what share holds it in this one. Darker bars: occupations where two-fifths or more of them do.

All 26 occupations: the industry’s fourteen largest hold 59.6% of its national employment, and every metro pays the six degree occupations further under the nation than the fourteen high-school-majority ones. Those are two different fourteens: four of the largest occupations are not high-school-majority jobs. Pick an occupation to follow its row across the charts.

What each pays Northeast Ohio against the nation

The pay gap against the nation is widest at the top of the schooling ladder

Here the page changes scope: from this section on, the dots are Northeast Ohio metros and the diamonds are the same job nationally. Those floor jobs are paid near the national rate in Akron and Cleveland. The shortfall concentrates where the region claims distinction: in every metro, the six occupations whose workers mostly hold degrees sit further under their national medians than the fourteen high-school-majority occupations. The median is the middle wage, half the jobs in that metro paying more and half less. Only 5 of Akron’s 25 published occupations are paid above the same job nationally, 11 of 25 in Cleveland, 5 of 22 in Canton-Massillon and 1 of 21 in Youngstown-Warren. Why the discount lands on exactly these jobs is a question these files cannot answer.

The degree band sits deeper under its diamonds than the floor band in every metro

Annual median wage, May 2024, across every industry that employs the occupation. One row is one job: the hollow diamond is what it pays nationally, and a metro’s dot to the LEFT of that diamond means the metro pays the job less than the country does. A thirty-dollar gap and a sixteen-thousand-dollar gap look alike at this scale, so each row also carries its metro median divided by the national one, printed to the right of the dots and headed with the metro it belongs to: 0.93 is 7% under the nation, 1.01 is 1% over. Rows are grouped by the schooling workers report (next section) and ordered by the national median inside each group, and each band’s summary is the middle of that band’s own ratios, never an average of dollars.

What the gap means in a paycheck Akron against the nation

Thirty dollars at the bottom of the ladder, sixteen thousand at the top

A molding-machine setter in metro Akron makes $41,260 a year at the median, thirty dollars over the same job’s national median. A chemical engineer in the same metro makes $105,660 against $121,860 nationally, about $16,200 a year under. That $16,200 is 13% of the national figure, wider than the 7% written on the pay chart’s degree band, because 7% is the middle of Akron’s six degree occupations and chemical engineers are the second-widest of the six. These are medians across every employer in each metro, not offers from any one plant, and they are the whole page in two paychecks.

What schooling the work reports United States

Six of these occupations are degree jobs; fourteen are high-school-majority; the rest sit between

Scope changes back: this survey has no Ohio cut, so these are national figures used to sort the rows on both charts. The degree band above is defined by what workers themselves report. Each bar splits one occupation’s surveyed workers and occupational experts by the education they say the job requires. The label at the right is the occupation’s Job Zone, the Labor Department’s rating of how much overall preparation the work needs, running from 1 for the least to 5 for the most. The six degree-majority occupations are the engineer and scientist jobs, exactly the rows the metros pay furthest under the nation.

Every degree-majority occupation is an engineer or scientist job

Share of respondents reporting each level as what the job requires, from O*NET 30.3, the Labor Department’s occupational database, which has no Ohio cut: these are national figures. Every bar runs the full width, so what varies is where the dark begins: the earlier it begins, the more schooling the job’s own people say it takes. The notch on each bar is that point, where the degree levels start, and a notch left of the half-way rule is a degree-majority job. Rows in the same order as the pay chart, and the shaded band holds the same six degree occupations. The federal release reports the two lowest Job Zones as one band, so no row reads Zone 1 or Zone 2 alone, and the bands come from the education split rather than the Zone, so the two need not agree.

Where the degree jobs are trained Northeast Ohio

Two universities confer the polymer degrees, and the count has fallen below half its old pace

The degree jobs the metros underpay draw their local supply from two universities, and a third confers the adjacent materials degrees. The federal completions record shows polymer programs conferring between 118 and 179 degrees a year from 2014 through 2021, an average of 139, then 54 in 2022 and 63 in 2023. Half that average is 70 a year, which is the rule drawn on the chart, and both later years sit under it. Two years is still a short record, and a program recoded to a different federal program code would leave these counts without leaving the region. The adjacent materials programs, meaning materials science and materials engineering at the same universities, held their usual range through the same two years.

Polymer degrees fell below half their 2014–2021 pace

Degrees conferred a year by the region’s three universities, polymer programs against the adjacent materials programs carried as a comparison group. The rule is half the 139-a-year polymer average for 2014–2021, so a polymer column under it is a year the region trained less than half as many as it did through 2021.

The University of Akron confers two-thirds of the region’s polymer degrees

Degrees conferred a year, average of 2021–2023, by program and institution. Darker bars: polymer programs; lighter: adjacent materials programs. Only Akron and Case Western confer polymer degrees; Kent State appears here among the materials programs alone.

The floor can staff from the whole country. The top has to be paid and trained here, and the training has fallen by half.

Akron pays its high-school-majority occupations a median 3% under the national rate and its degree occupations 7% under it, or 0.97× and 0.93× the national medians. The polymer programs behind those degree jobs conferred 63 degrees in 2023, against an average of 139 a year through 2021, and two years is a short record. Both findings point the same way for an employer here: the occupations the region is known for are the ones it pays furthest under the rate, and the ones its universities now confer fewest of. Whether that is a shortage or a correction, these files cannot say.
