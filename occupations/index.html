Staffing, pay and degrees

Half the nation’s molding-machine setters work in this one industry.

Can the region staff, pay and replace the jobs that make it a polymer capital? The federal record answers one occupation at a time: the industry runs mostly on floor work, and all four Northeast Ohio metros pay the degree occupations under the national rate.

By John Swanson, analysis and graphics · Data BLS, O*NET and IPEDS · August 2026

The staffing pattern

Fourteen occupations are 59% of the industry, and most of them are on the floor

Start with who does the work. Each bar is one occupation’s share of everyone employed in plastics and rubber manufacturing nationally in 2024, and the figure at the bar’s end asks the reverse question: what share of that occupation, across every industry, works in this one. That reverse share is how polymer-specific a job is, and the two most polymer-specific jobs on the list are floor jobs.

All 26 occupations: the industry’s fourteen largest hold 59.4% of its national employment, and every metro pays the six degree occupations further under the nation than the fourteen high-school ones. Pick an occupation to follow its row across the charts.

What each pays

The pay gap against the nation is widest at the top of the schooling ladder

Those floor jobs are paid near the national rate in Akron and Cleveland. The shortfall concentrates where the region claims distinction: in every metro, the six occupations whose workers mostly hold degrees sit further under their national medians than the fourteen high-school occupations. Only 5 of Akron’s 25 published occupations beat the same job nationally, 10 of 25 in Cleveland, 5 of 22 in Canton-Massillon and 1 of 21 in Youngstown-Warren. Why the discount lands on exactly these jobs is a question these files cannot answer.

What the gap means in a paycheck

Thirty dollars at the bottom of the ladder, sixteen thousand at the top

A molding-machine setter in metro Akron makes $41,260 a year at the median, thirty dollars over the same job’s national median. A chemical engineer in the same metro makes $105,660 against $121,860 nationally, about $16,200 a year under. These are medians across every employer in each metro, not offers from any one plant, and they are the whole page in two paychecks.

What schooling the work reports

Six of these occupations are degree jobs; fourteen are high-school jobs; the rest sit between

The degree band above is defined by what workers themselves report. Each bar splits one occupation’s surveyed workers and occupational experts by the education they say the job requires, from high school or less to a graduate degree. The label at the right is the occupation’s Job Zone, the Labor Department’s rating of how much overall preparation the work typically needs. The six degree-majority occupations are the engineer and scientist jobs, exactly the rows the metros pay furthest under the nation.

Where the degree jobs are trained here

Three universities confer the degrees, and the polymer count has fallen by more than half

The degree jobs the metros underpay draw their local supply from three schools. The federal completions record shows polymer programs conferring between 118 and 179 degrees a year from 2014 through 2021, then 54 in 2022 and 63 in 2023. Two years is still a short record, and a program recoded to a different federal program code would leave these counts without leaving the region. The adjacent materials programs held their usual range through the same two years.

The region staffs from the floor, pays the top under the nation, and now trains half as many.

Akron pays its high-school occupations a median 0.97× the national rate and its degree occupations 0.93×. The polymer programs behind those degree jobs conferred 63 degrees in 2023, against 118 to 179 a year through 2021, and two years is a short record.
