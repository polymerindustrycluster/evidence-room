Polymer Industry Cluster Cluster vitals · occupations

Staffing, pay and degrees, occupation by occupation · BLS, O*NET and IPEDS, 2023–2024

In plastics and rubber manufacturing, one job in nine is a molding-machine setter, and half of all the nation’s molding-machine setters work in this one industry.

Can the region staff, pay and replace the jobs that make it a polymer capital? The federal record answers one occupation at a time: the industry mostly runs on floor work, all four Northeast Ohio metros pay the degree occupations under the national rate while paying the floor closer to it, and the local polymer-degree count has run at less than half its earlier pace for two years (wage figures are all-industry medians, not plastics-plant pay).

The staffing pattern

Fourteen occupations are 59% of the industry, and most of them are on the floor

Start with who does the work. Each bar is one occupation’s share of everyone employed in plastics and rubber manufacturing nationally in 2024, and the figure at the bar’s end asks the reverse question: what share of that occupation, across every industry, works in this one. That reverse share is how polymer-specific a job is, and the two most polymer-specific jobs on the list are floor jobs.

What each pays

The pay gap against the nation is widest at the top of the schooling ladder

Those floor jobs are paid near the national rate in Akron and Cleveland. The shortfall concentrates where the region claims distinction: in every metro, the six occupations whose workers mostly hold degrees sit further under their national medians than the fourteen high-school occupations. Only 5 of Akron’s 25 published occupations beat the same job nationally, 10 of 25 in Cleveland, 5 of 22 in Canton-Massillon and 1 of 21 in Youngstown-Warren. Why the discount lands on exactly these jobs is a question these files cannot answer.

What the gap means in a paycheck

Thirty dollars at the bottom of the ladder, sixteen thousand at the top

A molding-machine setter in metro Akron makes $41,260 a year at the median, thirty dollars over the same job’s national median. A chemical engineer in the same metro makes $105,660 against $121,860 nationally, about $16,200 a year under. These are medians across every employer in each metro, not offers from any one plant, and they are the whole page in two paychecks.

Where the degree jobs are trained here

Three universities confer the degrees, and the polymer count has fallen by more than half

The degree jobs the metros underpay draw their local supply from three schools. The federal completions record shows polymer programs conferring between 118 and 179 degrees a year from 2014 through 2021, then 54 in 2022 and 63 in 2023. Two years is still a short record, 2024 was not yet available from the API when this was built, and a program recoded to a different federal program code would leave these counts without leaving the region. The adjacent materials programs held their usual range through the same two years.

The industry’s jobs are mostly floor jobs, and Akron and Cleveland pay those within a few percent of the national rate. The degree jobs are few, pay under the national rate in every metro, and their local polymer-degree count has halved.
